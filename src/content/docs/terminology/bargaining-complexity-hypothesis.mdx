---
title: Bargaining Complexity Hypothesis
description: The theory that coalitions with more members face more complex negotiations and therefore shorter lifespans.
---

The **bargaining complexity hypothesis** is one of two major explanations for why some coalition governments last longer than others (the other being the [ideological diversity hypothesis](/terminology/ideological-diversity-hypothesis/)). It holds that the more parties in a coalition, the harder it is to maintain agreement — and therefore the shorter the coalition's expected lifespan.

## Core Logic

The mechanism is straightforward: each additional coalition partner adds another set of preferences, another veto point, and another potential source of disagreement. As the number of partners grows, the bargaining space becomes more complex, the probability of impasse increases, and the chances of finding mutually acceptable compromises on new issues decreases.

| Number of Partners | Bargaining Complexity | Predicted Duration |
|---|---|---|
| 2 (minimum coalition) | Low | Long |
| 3–4 | Moderate | Medium |
| 5+ (oversized coalition) | High | Short |

## Origins

The hypothesis is most closely associated with [Lawrence Dodd's *Coalitions in Parliamentary Government* (1976)](/papers/dodd-coalitions-parliamentary-government/), who argued that **minimum winning coalitions** — those with just enough seats for a majority — tend to last longest. As coalitions add surplus members, each additional partner increases the complexity of internal negotiation without adding to the coalition's winning margin.

Earlier work by Taylor and Herman (1971) had documented an empirical correlation between the number of coalition parties and shorter cabinet duration, which Dodd formalized into a theoretical explanation.

## The Debate

The bargaining complexity hypothesis has been challenged by the [ideological diversity hypothesis](/terminology/ideological-diversity-hypothesis/), which argues that it's not the *number* of partners that matters but the *ideological range* they span. A three-party coalition of ideologically similar parties might last longer than a two-party coalition of ideological opposites.

[Warwick (1979)](/papers/warwick-durability-coalition-governments/) found that ideological range was a stronger predictor of coalition termination than the simple number of parties, lending support to the diversity hypothesis. However, the two variables are correlated (more parties typically means wider ideological range), making it difficult to fully disentangle their effects.

| Hypothesis | Key Variable | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Bargaining Complexity** | Number of coalition partners | More veto points, harder to coordinate |
| **Ideological Diversity** | Ideological range of coalition | Greater policy disagreement, more internal conflict |

## Current Status

Empirical research has generally found support for both hypotheses, with their relative importance varying across countries and time periods. Many scholars now view them as complementary rather than competing: more parties *and* wider ideological range both contribute to coalition fragility, likely through overlapping but distinct mechanisms.
