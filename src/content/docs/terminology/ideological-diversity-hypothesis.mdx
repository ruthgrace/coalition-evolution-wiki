---
title: Ideological Diversity Hypothesis
description: The theory that coalitions spanning a wider ideological range are more fragile and shorter-lived.
---

The **ideological diversity hypothesis** holds that the key predictor of coalition duration is not the number of partners but the **ideological range** the coalition spans. Coalitions whose members hold similar policy positions can more easily maintain agreement; coalitions that bridge large ideological gaps face constant internal conflict and are more likely to collapse.

## Core Logic

Every policy decision a coalition faces is a potential source of disagreement. The wider the ideological distance between coalition partners, the more frequently they will disagree, the harder compromise becomes, and the more likely that a specific issue will trigger a coalition crisis. Ideological range acts as a measure of the coalition's **latent conflict potential**.

| Ideological Range | Internal Conflict | Predicted Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow (adjacent parties) | Low | Long |
| Moderate | Medium | Medium |
| Wide (spanning left to right) | High | Short |

## Origins

The hypothesis is most closely associated with [Paul Warwick's 1979 study](../../papers/warwick-durability-coalition-governments/) of coalition government durability in Western European parliamentary democracies. Warwick found that ideological range was a **stronger predictor** of coalition termination than the simple number of coalition parties, challenging the [bargaining complexity hypothesis](../../terminology/bargaining-complexity-hypothesis/) associated with [Dodd (1976)](../../papers/dodd-coalitions-parliamentary-government/).

The insight also connects to the policy-based coalition formation theories of [Axelrod (1970)](../../papers/axelrod-conflict-of-interest/) and [De Swaan (1973)](../../papers/de-swaan-policy-distance-theory/), which predict that coalitions form among ideologically adjacent parties precisely *because* ideological proximity promotes coalition stability.

## Measurement Challenges

A practical difficulty with the hypothesis is **how to measure ideological range**. Different approaches can yield different results:

| Measurement Method | Source | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Expert surveys | Political scientists rate party positions | Subjective; may reflect stereotypes |
| Party manifestos | Coding of election platforms (e.g., Manifesto Project) | Platforms may not reflect governing behavior |
| Roll-call votes | Voting patterns in parliament | Only available where roll-call data exists |
| Self-placement surveys | Party elites or voters report their own positions | May differ from revealed preferences |

The choice of measurement can affect whether the ideological diversity hypothesis outperforms the bargaining complexity hypothesis in empirical tests.

## Relationship to Bargaining Complexity

The ideological diversity and bargaining complexity hypotheses are often presented as rivals, but they are partially correlated: coalitions with more parties tend to span wider ideological ranges. This makes it difficult to cleanly separate their effects. Most recent empirical work treats them as **complementary** — both the number of partners and the ideological spread contribute to coalition fragility through overlapping but distinct channels.

[Laver and Shepsle (1996)](../../papers/laver-shepsle-making-breaking-governments/) offered a different framing that partially subsumes both: coalitions collapse when the political landscape shifts enough to make an alternative portfolio allocation viable to a parliamentary majority. In this framework, both more parties and wider ideological range make it *easier* for such an alternative to emerge.
