Mancur Olson and Richard Zeckhauser's 1966 paper "An Economic Theory of Alliances" applied collective action theory to international coalitions, producing one of the most cited and debated findings in alliance studies: the **exploitation of the great by the small**. In any alliance providing a collective good (like security), smaller members have an incentive to free-ride on the contributions of the largest member, who bears a disproportionate share of the burden because it has the most to lose from the alliance's failure.

The paper was written with NATO specifically in mind, and its predictions matched the empirical pattern remarkably well: the United States consistently spent a larger share of GDP on defense than its European allies. This finding has shaped decades of political debate about NATO burden-sharing — from the Cold War through to the present — and provides a structural explanation for why coalition tensions over contributions are not aberrations but inherent features of alliance dynamics.

The framework also helps explain why coalitions evolve over time. As the largest member grows frustrated with free-riding, it may demand reforms, threaten withdrawal, or restructure the alliance. Smaller members, meanwhile, have rational incentives to resist increased contributions. This dynamic creates a persistent internal tension that shapes coalition evolution independently of external threats.

## Background

Olson and Zeckhauser built on Olson's earlier *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965), which showed that groups providing public goods face systematic free-rider problems. They extended this logic to alliances:

- Alliance defense is a **public good**: once provided, all members benefit regardless of their contribution
- The largest member values the good most (in absolute terms) and will provide it even if others contribute nothing
- Smaller members rationally under-contribute, knowing the good will be provided anyway

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Exploitation of the Great by the Small** | The largest alliance member bears a disproportionate burden |
| **Defense as Public Good** | Alliance security benefits all members regardless of individual contribution |
| **Free-Riding** | Smaller members under-contribute relative to their capacity |
| **Suboptimal Provision** | Total alliance defense spending is lower than the collectively optimal level |

## NATO Burden-Sharing Data

The theory's predictions have been borne out across decades of NATO data:

| Period | US Defense Spending (% GDP) | European NATO Average (% GDP) | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold War (1960s–80s) | 6–9% | 3–4% | Large |
| Post–Cold War (1990s–2000s) | 3–4% | 1.5–2% | Persistent |
| Post-2014 (Wales Summit pledge) | 3.4% | ~1.5–2% (rising) | Narrowing slowly |
| 2% target compliance (2024) | Exceeds 2% | ~23 of 31 members meet target | Improving |

## Open Questions

- **Is alliance defense really a pure public good?** If defense has "private" benefits (protecting specific territories), the free-riding prediction weakens. Subsequent scholars have debated the public vs. private goods mix.
- **Do burden-sharing disputes actually threaten alliance cohesion?** Despite persistent free-riding, NATO has endured for 75+ years. Institutional mechanisms may compensate for collective action problems.
- **How do burden-sharing dynamics change as threats change?** Russia's invasion of Ukraine produced a dramatic increase in European defense spending, suggesting that acute threats can temporarily overcome free-riding incentives.
