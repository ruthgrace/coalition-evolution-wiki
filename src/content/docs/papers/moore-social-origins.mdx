Barrington Moore Jr.'s *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy: Lord and Peasant in the Making of the Modern World* (1966) is one of the most influential works of comparative historical sociology. Moore argued that the political trajectories of major nations — whether they became democracies, fascist states, or communist regimes — were determined by the **coalitions formed among three key classes**: the landed aristocracy, the bourgeoisie (urban middle class), and the peasantry.

The book's famous dictum — **"no bourgeois, no democracy"** — captures its central argument: democracy emerged only where a strong, independent bourgeoisie existed to challenge the landed elite. Where the bourgeoisie was weak and allied with traditional elites against the lower classes, fascism resulted. Where the peasantry was the revolutionary force and the bourgeoisie was absent, communism emerged.

Moore's work predates and deeply influenced [Luebbert's (1991)](/papers/luebbert-liberalism-fascism-social-democracy/) analysis of interwar Europe, which refined and extended the class coalition framework. Together, they form the backbone of the comparative historical approach to understanding how coalitions of power determine political outcomes at the highest level.

## Background

Moore analyzed eight countries through detailed historical case studies:

| Country | Path | Key Coalition |
|---|---|---|
| England | Democratic | Bourgeoisie breaks the power of the landed aristocracy |
| France | Democratic (revolutionary) | Bourgeoisie and peasantry overthrow the old regime |
| United States | Democratic | No feudal aristocracy to overcome; bourgeois hegemony from the start |
| Germany | Fascist | Weak bourgeoisie allies with Junker aristocracy; "revolution from above" |
| Japan | Fascist | Modernizing elite co-opts commercial class; peasantry suppressed |
| Russia | Communist | Weak bourgeoisie; peasant revolution co-opted by revolutionary party |
| China | Communist | Massive peasant mobilization against both landlords and weak bourgeoisie |
| India | Democratic | Partial; complex caste/class dynamics produce a fragile democratic path |

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Three Routes to Modernity** | Democratic, fascist, and communist paths determined by class coalitions during industrialization |
| **No Bourgeois, No Democracy** | An independent commercial/industrial middle class is a necessary condition for democracy |
| **Role of the Peasantry** | Peasant revolution leads to communism; peasant suppression enables fascism; peasant marginalization allows democracy |
| **Revolution from Above** | In Germany and Japan, modernization was led by traditional elites allying with a weak bourgeoisie, producing authoritarian modernization |

## Three Routes to the Modern World

| Route | Class Configuration | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Bourgeois Revolution** | Strong bourgeoisie challenges aristocracy; peasantry marginalized or co-opted | Liberal democracy (England, France, US) |
| **Revolution from Above** | Weak bourgeoisie allies with aristocracy; peasantry suppressed | Fascism (Germany, Japan) |
| **Peasant Revolution** | Weak bourgeoisie; massive peasant mobilization; revolutionary party seizes power | Communism (Russia, China) |

## Open Questions

- **Is "no bourgeois, no democracy" still true?** Many post-colonial democracies emerged without a strong indigenous bourgeoisie. Does the thesis need updating for the late 20th and 21st centuries?
- **How much does Moore's framework rely on European exceptionalism?** The cases are overwhelmingly European or European-influenced. Do the same class dynamics drive political outcomes in Africa, the Middle East, or Southeast Asia?
- **What about the role of the state itself?** Moore focuses on social classes but gives less attention to state capacity and autonomy as independent variables — a gap later addressed by Theda Skocpol and others.
