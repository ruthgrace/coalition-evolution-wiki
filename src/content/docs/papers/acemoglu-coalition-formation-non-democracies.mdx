Daron Acemoglu, Georgy Egorov, and Konstantin Sonin's 2008 paper "Coalition Formation in Non-Democracies" brought formal coalition theory to authoritarian politics — a domain where coalition dynamics are arguably even more consequential than in democracies, since the stakes include not just policy but survival. Their central finding is counter-intuitive: in dictatorships, the *most powerful* members of a ruling coalition are the most likely to be purged, precisely because they pose the greatest threat.

This result inverts the logic of democratic coalition theory, where powerful partners are valued for the resources they bring. In authoritarian settings, power within the coalition is a double-edged sword: a strong ally helps the ruling group win, but also has the capability to seize power for themselves. The model explains a recurring pattern in authoritarian regimes — from Stalin's purges to Saddam Hussein's inner circle — where leaders systematically eliminate their most capable associates.

The paper demonstrates that coalition dynamics in non-democracies follow predictable structural patterns, even in the absence of formal institutions like elections or parliamentary votes. The "rules of the game" are different, but the logic of coalition formation, maintenance, and dissolution still applies.

## Background

The model considers a group of individuals with differing power levels who must form a ruling coalition. The ruling coalition can then choose to eliminate (purge) some of its own members to redistribute power. The key dynamic:

1. The ruling coalition forms to control the state
2. Members of the coalition consider whether eliminating a powerful member would improve their position
3. If a majority within the coalition prefers to purge a member, that member is eliminated
4. The process iterates until a stable coalition is reached

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Endogenous Purges** | Ruling coalitions can eliminate their own members to redistribute power |
| **Power as Threat** | The most powerful coalition members are the most dangerous — and thus most likely to be purged |
| **Stable Coalitions** | Equilibrium coalitions emerge when no remaining subset wants to eliminate another member |
| **Weakness as Safety** | Weaker members may survive precisely because they are not threatening |

## Predictions vs. Democratic Coalition Theory

| Feature | Democratic Coalitions | Authoritarian Coalitions |
|---|---|---|
| **Value of Powerful Partners** | High (bring votes, resources) | Ambiguous (bring strength but also threat) |
| **Coalition Exit** | Voluntary withdrawal, elections | Purge, exile, execution |
| **Stability Mechanism** | Institutional rules, elections | Balance of threat within the coalition |
| **Payoff Distribution** | Proportional to resources (Gamson's Law) | Skewed toward the leader; others get survival |
| **Information** | Relatively transparent | Opaque; deception and signaling are central |

## Historical Examples

The model's predictions map onto well-known episodes of authoritarian coalition dynamics:

| Regime | Pattern | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **Stalin's USSR (1930s)** | Systematic purges of powerful Old Bolsheviks | Eliminated the most capable potential rivals |
| **Saddam's Iraq** | Repeated purges of military and party leaders | Removed anyone strong enough to mount a coup |
| **Mao's China** | Cultural Revolution targeted senior party officials | Destroyed the coalition that could constrain Mao's power |
| **Post-revolution Iran** | Elimination of secular and leftist allies after 1979 | Khomeini's coalition narrowed once power was secured |

## Open Questions

- **What determines the initial ruling coalition?** The model takes the set of potential coalition members as given; in practice, how authoritarian coalitions first form is itself a puzzle.
- **How do institutions constrain authoritarian coalition dynamics?** Even dictatorships have some institutional structures (parties, militaries, courts). How do these shape the purge logic?
- **Can the framework explain coalition *expansion* in autocracies?** The model focuses on coalition narrowing through purges, but some authoritarian regimes co-opt new members over time.
