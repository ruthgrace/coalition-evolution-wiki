Paul Sabatier and Hank Jenkins-Smith's **Advocacy Coalition Framework (ACF)**, developed across several publications culminating in their 1993 book *Policy Change and Learning*, offers a distinctive approach to coalition dynamics: coalitions not of parties or states, but of **policy actors** united by shared beliefs. In any policy domain, the ACF identifies competing advocacy coalitions — networks of politicians, bureaucrats, interest groups, researchers, and journalists who share core beliefs about how the world works and what should be done.

The ACF's central insight is that these belief-based coalitions are remarkably stable over time. People rarely change their deep core beliefs, so coalition membership is sticky — often persisting for a decade or more. Policy change happens not because people change their minds, but through distinct pathways that the framework has identified over successive revisions: the original 1993 version emphasized **policy-oriented learning** (gradual accumulation of evidence) and **external shocks** (elections, economic crises, disasters). Later revisions added **negotiated agreements** between coalitions (~1998) and **internal shocks** within the subsystem (2007), bringing the current ACF to four pathways of policy change.

This framework bridges coalition theory and policy studies, showing that the dynamics of coalition formation and evolution operate not just in legislatures and international systems but in the more diffuse world of policy subsystems. It has been applied to hundreds of policy domains across dozens of countries, from environmental regulation to education to health care.

## Background

The ACF models policy subsystems as arenas of competition between two or more advocacy coalitions. Each coalition is organized around a hierarchical belief system:

| Belief Level | Description | Susceptibility to Change |
|---|---|---|
| **Deep Core Beliefs** | Fundamental normative axioms (e.g., the role of government, human nature) | Very resistant — rarely change |
| **Policy Core Beliefs** | Basic positions on the policy domain (e.g., environmental protection vs. economic growth) | Resistant — change only with major shocks |
| **Secondary Aspects** | Instrumental decisions about implementation, specific regulations | Moderately susceptible to learning and evidence |

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Advocacy Coalitions** | Networks of actors sharing core policy beliefs who coordinate to influence policy |
| **Belief System Hierarchy** | Deep core, policy core, and secondary beliefs — each with different change dynamics |
| **Policy-Oriented Learning** | Gradual, evidence-based shifts in secondary beliefs within and between coalitions |
| **External Shocks** | Major events (elections, crises) that redistribute resources or attention, enabling policy core changes |
| **Policy Brokers** | Actors who mediate between coalitions and facilitate compromise |

## How Coalitions Change Over Time

The ACF predicts that coalition evolution follows specific patterns:

| Mechanism | Speed | What Changes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Policy-Oriented Learning** | Slow (years/decades) | Secondary beliefs, instrumental strategies | Scientific evidence gradually shifts regulatory approaches |
| **External Shocks** | Fast | Policy core beliefs, coalition membership | A major oil spill reshuffles the environmental policy coalition |
| **Negotiated Agreements** | Medium | Policy positions (not beliefs) | Opposing coalitions compromise on specific regulations |
| **Internal Shocks** | Fast | Policy core beliefs, coalition resources | A major policy failure discredits the dominant coalition |

## Open Questions

- **How do advocacy coalitions relate to partisan coalitions?** The ACF focuses on policy subsystems, but advocacy coalitions often map onto (and are shaped by) broader party coalitions.
- **Does the framework work in non-democratic settings?** Most ACF research has been conducted in democracies where policy subsystems are relatively open. Authoritarian regimes may have different dynamics.
- **How has social media changed advocacy coalitions?** The rise of online activism may be creating more fluid, less stable coalitions than the ACF's decade-long stability assumption suggests.
