A.F.K. Organski's **power transition theory**, first published in *World Politics* (1958), offers a structural explanation for why major wars occur and how the international hierarchy of power shifts over time. The theory's central claim inverts the conventional wisdom: it is not a *balance* of power that preserves peace, but an *imbalance*. War becomes most likely when a rising, dissatisfied power approaches parity with the dominant state in the international system.

Power transition theory has profound implications for understanding coalition dynamics at the international level. The dominant power typically leads a coalition of satisfied states that benefit from the existing international order. A rising challenger may form a countervailing coalition of dissatisfied states. The moment of maximum danger is the "transition" — when the challenger's power crosses that of the dominant state.

The theory has been applied to numerous historical episodes (the rise of Germany before both World Wars, the Cold War US-Soviet rivalry) and is now widely invoked in debates about the US-China relationship. It remains one of the most cited frameworks in international relations for understanding long-run shifts in global power coalitions.

## Background

Organski proposed a hierarchical model of the international system:

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| **Dominant Power** | The most powerful state, which shapes the international order |
| **Great Powers** | Powerful states that may be satisfied or dissatisfied with the status quo |
| **Middle Powers** | States with regional influence |
| **Small Powers** | States with limited capacity to shape the system |

The key dynamic is between the dominant power and a rising great power. If the rising power is *satisfied* with the existing order, the transition can be peaceful. If it is *dissatisfied*, war becomes likely.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Hierarchy, Not Anarchy** | The international system is hierarchically ordered, not anarchic as realists assume |
| **Parity = Danger** | War is most likely when the rising power approaches parity with the dominant power |
| **Dissatisfaction** | The challenger must be dissatisfied with the status quo for conflict to occur |
| **Coalition Alignment** | Satisfied powers ally with the dominant state; dissatisfied powers may coalesce around the challenger |

## Historical Applications

| Transition | Dominant Power | Challenger | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late 19th century | Britain | Germany | World War I |
| Interwar period | Britain/France | Germany (again) | World War II |
| Cold War | United States | Soviet Union | Managed rivalry, no direct war |
| 21st century | United States | China | Ongoing |

## Open Questions

- **Can power transitions be managed peacefully?** The US overtook Britain in the late 19th century without war, suggesting satisfaction with the existing order matters more than parity alone.
- **How do coalitions form around challengers?** The theory says dissatisfied states may align with the rising power, but the mechanisms of counter-coalition formation are underdeveloped.
- **Is the theory too focused on dyadic rivalry?** Real international systems involve many great powers and complex multilateral coalitions, not just a dominant-challenger pair.
