Bruce Bueno de Mesquita, Alastair Smith, Randolph Siverson, and James Morrow's *The Logic of Political Survival* (2003) introduced **selectorate theory**, one of the most influential frameworks for understanding how coalition size shapes governance across all regime types. The core premise is simple and powerful: a leader's primary goal is to stay in power, and the strategy for doing so depends on the size of the **winning coalition** — the group of people whose support the leader needs to retain power.

In democracies, the winning coalition is large (a majority of voters), so leaders must provide **public goods** — infrastructure, education, rule of law — to satisfy enough people. In dictatorships, the winning coalition is small (a handful of generals, party elites, or oligarchs), so leaders provide **private goods** — cash, luxury, patronage — to buy the loyalty of the few people who matter.

This framework unifies the study of democratic and authoritarian politics under a single theory of coalition maintenance. It explains not just different governance outcomes but also why leaders in small-coalition systems are more durable (cheaper to maintain a small coalition) and why transitions to democracy are so difficult (expanding the coalition threatens incumbent elites).

## Background

Selectorate theory identifies three nested groups:

| Group | Description | Example (Democracy) | Example (Dictatorship) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nominal Selectorate** | Everyone with some formal say in choosing the leader | All registered voters | All party members |
| **Real Selectorate** | Those whose support actually matters | Active voters in competitive districts | Senior military and party officials |
| **Winning Coalition** | The minimal subset whose support keeps the leader in power | A majority of voters | A handful of key elites |

The key variable is the **ratio of winning coalition to selectorate**. When this ratio is small (dictatorship), leaders can cheaply buy loyalty with private goods. When it's large (democracy), private goods become too expensive and leaders must invest in public goods.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Winning Coalition Size** | The primary variable explaining governance differences across regimes |
| **Public vs. Private Goods** | Large coalitions get public goods; small coalitions get private payoffs |
| **Leader Survival** | Leaders with small winning coalitions last longer (cheaper to maintain) |
| **Loyalty Norm** | Members of small winning coalitions are loyal because being excluded is costly — there's always someone to replace them from the selectorate |

## Empirical Predictions

| Winning Coalition Size | Public Goods Provision | Leader Tenure | Corruption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small (autocracy) | Low | Long | High |
| Large (democracy) | High | Shorter (but with peaceful transitions) | Lower |

## Open Questions

- **How do you measure winning coalition size empirically?** The theory's predictions are clear, but operationalizing "winning coalition" across different regime types is challenging.
- **What about hybrid regimes?** Many contemporary states are neither clearly democratic nor clearly authoritarian. How does selectorate theory handle the gray zone?
- **Does the theory explain regime transitions?** The framework is primarily about equilibrium behavior within a regime type, not about transitions between types.
