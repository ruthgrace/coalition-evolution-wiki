Lawrence Dodd's *Coalitions in Parliamentary Government* (1976) tackled a question that earlier coalition theorists had largely set aside: not just which coalitions form, but which ones *last*. Dodd challenged the conventional wisdom that durable cabinets require single-party majority government, arguing instead that coalition cabinets can be stable — provided they achieve minimum winning status.

Dodd's central claim is that cabinet durability depends on the coalitional status of the governing parties, which is itself shaped by the fractionalization, instability, and polarization of the parliamentary party system. Cabinets that are minimum winning tend to endure; as they depart from that status (becoming either undersized minority governments or oversized surplus coalitions), durability decreases.

The book sparked a major debate in comparative politics between two rival explanations of coalition instability: the **bargaining complexity hypothesis** (more parties = harder to maintain agreement) and the **ideological diversity hypothesis** (wider ideological spread = more internal conflict). This debate continues to shape empirical research on coalition survival.

## Background

Dodd built on Riker's size principle but shifted the dependent variable from coalition *formation* to coalition *survival*. His key innovation was connecting party system characteristics to cabinet outcomes:

- **Fractionalization**: More fragmented party systems produce more complex coalitions
- **Instability**: Volatile electoral environments undermine coalition agreements
- **Polarization**: Ideologically extreme party systems reduce the set of viable coalition partners

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Minimum Winning Status** | Coalitions that are just large enough to hold a majority tend to last longest |
| **Bargaining Complexity** | More coalition partners means more veto players and more potential for disagreement |
| **Party System Properties** | Fractionalization, instability, and polarization shape which coalitions are possible and how long they last |
| **Durability as Dependent Variable** | Shifts focus from which coalition forms to how long it survives |

## The Durability Debate

Dodd's work set up a lasting empirical debate:

| Hypothesis | Mechanism | Key Proponents |
|---|---|---|
| **Bargaining Complexity** | More parties in government = shorter duration | Dodd (1976), Taylor & Herman (1971) |
| **Ideological Diversity** | Wider ideological range = shorter duration | Warwick (1979), Laver & Shepsle (1996) |
| **Events-Based** | Exogenous shocks (economic crises, scandals) trigger termination | Browne, Frendreis & Gleiber (1986) |

Empirical tests have generally found support for both the complexity and diversity hypotheses, though their relative importance varies across countries and time periods.

## Open Questions

- **Are Dodd's predictions confirmed?** Riker's and Dodd's propositions about minimum winning coalitions have received mixed empirical support. Many durable governments are not minimum winning.
- **What role do institutions play?** Constructive votes of no confidence, investiture requirements, and dissolution powers all shape cabinet survival but fall outside Dodd's framework.
- **Does the bargaining complexity vs. ideological diversity debate have a resolution?** Or are both mechanisms operating simultaneously?
