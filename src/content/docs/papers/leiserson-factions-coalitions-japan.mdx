Michael Leiserson's 1968 article "Factions and Coalitions in One-Party Japan" demonstrated that coalition dynamics don't require multiple parties — they operate with equal force *inside* a dominant party. Using Japan's Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) as his case, Leiserson showed that the LDP's internal factions behaved exactly like parties in a multi-party parliament: forming minimum winning coalitions to control the party presidency (and thus the prime ministership), bargaining over cabinet portfolios, and dissolving and reforming as factional fortunes shifted.

This was an important extension of coalition theory's scope. The formal models of [Riker (1962)](/papers/riker-theory-of-political-coalitions/) and [Gamson (1961)](/papers/gamson-coalition-theory-resources/) were developed with multi-party systems in mind, but Leiserson demonstrated their applicability to **intra-party factional politics** — a domain that covers a large share of real-world political competition, particularly in dominant-party systems.

The LDP governed Japan almost continuously from 1955 to 2009. Throughout this period, the real political competition was not between parties but between LDP factions competing for the party presidency. Cabinet posts became the currency of coalition politics: rewards for supporting factions, inducements to wavering factions, and buffers to soften opposition from hostile factions.

## Background

The LDP has been composed of several relatively stable factions since its founding in 1955. These factions are the key actors in the biennial election of the party president, who becomes Prime Minister. A prime minister is chosen by a coalition of factions controlling a majority of votes at the party convention.

| Feature | Multi-Party Coalition | LDP Factional Coalition |
|---|---|---|
| **Actors** | Political parties | Intra-party factions |
| **Prize** | Government formation | Party presidency → Prime Minister |
| **Currency** | Cabinet portfolios | Cabinet portfolios |
| **Binding mechanism** | Coalition agreement | Factional loyalty, patronage |
| **Exit option** | Leave coalition, new elections | Switch factional allegiance |

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Factions as Parties** | LDP factions behave like parties in a multi-party system, forming coalitions to control leadership |
| **Minimum Winning Factional Coalitions** | The winning faction coalition tends toward the minimum size needed to control the party convention |
| **Cabinet as Payoff** | Cabinet posts are distributed to reward coalition factions and co-opt opponents |
| **Generalizability** | Coalition theory applies not just to inter-party competition but to intra-party factional politics |

## Open Questions

- **Do other dominant-party systems show the same pattern?** Mexico's PRI, India's Congress Party, and China's CCP all have internal factional dynamics. Do they follow coalition-theoretic predictions?
- **How do formal institutional rules (electoral systems, party rules) shape factional coalition dynamics?** Japan's electoral system changes in 1994 significantly altered factional behavior.
- **What happens when the dominant party loses power?** The LDP's brief loss of power in 1993 and 2009–2012 disrupted factional dynamics. How do factions behave during and after periods of opposition?
