In 1961, William Gamson proposed what would become one of the most robust empirical regularities in coalition research: the idea that participants in a coalition expect payoffs proportional to the resources they contribute. This "proportionality norm" — later formalized as **Gamson's Law** — has proven remarkably durable, particularly in predicting how parliamentary parties divide cabinet portfolios.

Gamson's contribution shifted the focus from *which* coalitions form (Riker's question) to *how the spoils are divided* once a coalition exists. His framework predicts that parties will favor the "cheapest winning coalition" — the one where their proportional share is maximized — because their own resources are fixed regardless of which coalition they join.

The theory has been tested extensively across European parliamentary democracies and, more recently, in non-European contexts. While deviations exist (small parties sometimes receive a bonus, formateur parties sometimes take a premium), the proportionality norm remains one of the strongest predictive results in coalition studies.

## Background

Gamson's core proposition:

> "Any participant will expect others to demand from a coalition a share of the payoff proportional to the amount of resources which they contribute to a coalition."

He proposed using a party's seat share as a proxy for resources and its share of cabinet ministries as a proxy for payoff. This operationalization turned an abstract theory into something directly testable.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Proportionality Norm** | Payoffs are distributed in proportion to contributed resources |
| **Cheapest Winning Coalition** | Actors prefer the winning coalition with the lowest total resources (maximizing their share) |
| **Resources as Bargaining Weight** | Seat share in parliament serves as the key resource metric |
| **Cabinet Portfolios as Payoff** | Ministry allocations are the observable outcome of coalition bargaining |

## Empirical Performance

Gamson's Law has been called the closest thing to a "law" in political science. A 2024 study tested its applicability globally and found it holds well beyond Western Europe:

| Context | Proportionality Fit |
|---|---|
| Western European parliaments | Strong |
| Central/Eastern European parliaments | Strong |
| Non-European parliamentary systems | Moderate to strong |
| Pre-election coalitions | Weaker (formateur bonus observed) |

## Open Questions

- **Why do small parties sometimes get a disproportionate bonus?** One explanation is that small parties are pivotal — without them, no majority — so they have outsized leverage.
- **Does Gamson's Law hold in non-democratic settings?** Cabinet allocation in authoritarian coalition regimes is understudied.
- **How does uncertainty and complexity affect proportionality?** Recent work by Falcó-Gimeno suggests that more complex coalition environments lead to stronger adherence to Gamson's Law as a simplifying heuristic.
