Abram de Swaan's *Coalition Theories and Cabinet Formations* (1973) advanced coalition theory by making policy distance the central variable. Where Axelrod required only that coalition partners be ideologically adjacent (minimal connected winning coalitions), De Swaan went further: parties seek to minimize the total policy *range* of the coalition, forming the narrowest possible winning coalition on the policy scale.

De Swaan's **closed minimal range theory** predicts that parliamentary coalitions will consist of ideologically adjacent parties spanning the smallest possible range on a policy scale while still holding a majority. This theory achieved strong empirical results, particularly in explaining coalition formation in Western European democracies during periods of political stability.

De Swaan's range-minimization approach was a significant advance over Axelrod's adjacency requirement. It allowed researchers to make sharper predictions by distinguishing among connected winning coalitions and selecting the one spanning the smallest policy distance. De Swaan's framework also laid the groundwork for later spatial models of coalition bargaining.

## Background

De Swaan modeled coalition formation as an n-person game of policy distance minimization:

> "An actor strives to bring about a winning coalition in which he is included and in which he expects to adopt a policy that is as close as possible, on a scale of policies, to his own most preferred policy."

The **closed minimal range** prediction holds that:
1. Coalition partners are adjacent on a socioeconomic policy scale
2. The coalition spans no wider an ideological range than necessary for a majority
3. No additional partner is included that would extend the range

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Policy Distance Minimization** | Actors seek coalitions whose expected policy output is closest to their ideal point |
| **Closed Coalition** | All parties between the two most extreme members are included |
| **Minimal Range** | The ideological spread of the coalition is as small as possible while maintaining a majority |
| **Distinction from Axelrod** | Predicts the *narrowest* connected winning coalition, not just any ideologically connected one |

## Empirical Performance

De Swaan tested his theory against historical coalition data from several European countries:

| Country | Closed Minimal Range Prediction Accuracy |
|---|---|
| Netherlands | High |
| Denmark | High |
| Sweden | Moderate–High |
| Italy | Moderate (complicated by anti-system parties) |
| France (Fourth Republic) | Moderate |

De Swaan also developed a classification of parties based on the number of closed minimal range coalitions they could participate in — effectively measuring a party's "coalitional options" and thus its structural power.

## Open Questions

- **How should party positions be measured?** De Swaan relied on expert judgments, but later work used manifesto coding, expert surveys, and roll-call analysis. Different measurement approaches can yield different predictions.
- **Does the theory work outside "normal" politics?** De Swaan noted his theory performed best during periods of stability; during crises, other dynamics may dominate.
- **What about non-policy motivations?** Office-seeking behavior (wanting ministries regardless of policy) can override policy distance considerations.
