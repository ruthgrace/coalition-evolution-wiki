Daniel Diermeier and Randy Stevenson's 1999 paper "Cabinet Survival and Competing Risks" introduced a crucial methodological refinement to the study of coalition duration: not all cabinet terminations are alike, and lumping them together produces misleading results. They distinguished between cabinets that end because of an **early election** (dissolution) and those that end because they are **replaced by a new cabinet** without an intervening election — and showed that these two types of termination have different causes and follow different patterns.

This matters for coalition theory because the mechanisms driving each type of termination are distinct. Strategic early elections may be called when the government expects to do well at the polls — a sign of strength, not weakness. Cabinet replacements without elections typically reflect internal coalition breakdown. Treating both as equivalent "failures" obscures these very different dynamics.

The paper brought **competing risks survival analysis** — a technique from biostatistics — into political science, and it has become the standard methodological approach for studying coalition duration. It also provided qualified support for strategic models of coalition termination, particularly those emphasizing the approach of mandatory elections as a key driver.

## Background

Previous studies of coalition duration, including [Warwick (1979)](../../papers/warwick-durability-coalition-governments/) and [Dodd (1976)](../../papers/dodd-coalitions-parliamentary-government/), treated all terminations as equivalent events. Diermeier and Stevenson showed this was problematic by identifying distinct terminal events:

| Termination Type | Mechanism | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Dissolution (early election)** | Government calls an election before the mandatory date | May reflect strategic timing, not coalition failure |
| **Replacement** | Government is replaced by a new cabinet without an election | Reflects genuine coalition breakdown |
| **Technical termination** | Mandatory election reached | Not a "failure" at all |

## Key Findings

As the next mandatory election approaches:

| Finding | Description |
|---|---|
| Probability of early election **increases** | Governments time elections strategically as the deadline nears |
| Risk of replacement may be **flat or decreasing** | Internal coalition dynamics don't necessarily worsen over time |
| Overall hazard **increases** | But this is driven primarily by the election-timing component |

This partially challenges [Warwick's (1979)](../../papers/warwick-durability-coalition-governments/) finding of a rising hazard rate: the rise may be driven by strategic election timing rather than increasing coalition fragility.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Competing Risks** | Different types of termination should be modeled separately |
| **Strategic Dissolution** | Early elections are often strategic choices, not coalition failures |
| **Mandatory Election Effects** | The approach of the mandatory election deadline drives much of the observed hazard pattern |
| **Selection Bias** | Which coalitions form in the first place affects how long they last — formation and duration are not independent |

## Open Questions

- **How do institutional rules shape the competing risks?** Countries with constructive votes of no confidence, fixed election dates, or different dissolution powers will show different patterns.
- **Can the framework be applied to non-parliamentary coalitions?** International alliances and social movement coalitions also "terminate" in multiple ways.
- **What about government reshuffles?** Some cabinet changes involve only a minor portfolio swap, not a full termination. Where should the line be drawn?
