Kaare Strom, Wolfgang Muller, and Torbjorn Bergman's *Cabinets and Coalition Bargaining: The Democratic Life Cycle in Western Europe* (2008) represents the most comprehensive empirical treatment of coalition politics ever assembled. Drawing on the largest dataset in coalition studies, the book examines every stage of the **coalition lifecycle** — from formation through governance to termination — across all Western European parliamentary democracies in the post-1945 period.

The book's central contribution is its insistence on a **dynamic, integrated approach**. Previous research had largely studied formation, governance, and termination as separate phenomena. Strom, Muller, and Bergman showed that these stages are deeply interconnected: how a coalition forms shapes how it governs, which in turn shapes when and how it ends. They championed bargaining and transaction cost theory as the unifying framework for understanding this lifecycle.

The project synthesized insights from nearly all the major theoretical traditions in coalition research — [Riker's](../../papers/riker-theory-of-political-coalitions/) size principle, [Axelrod's](../../papers/axelrod-conflict-of-interest/) ideological proximity, [Laver and Shepsle's](../../papers/laver-shepsle-making-breaking-governments/) portfolio allocation, [Warwick's](../../papers/warwick-durability-coalition-governments/) duration analysis — and tested them against a common dataset.

## Background

The book identifies six clusters of explanatory factors for coalition politics:

| Factor Cluster | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Country-specific circumstances** | Political culture, historical legacies |
| **Temporal context** | Economic conditions, international events |
| **Structural attributes** | Party system fragmentation, number of viable coalitions |
| **Actor preferences** | Policy positions, office motivations |
| **Institutions** | Investiture rules, dissolution powers, constructive no-confidence |
| **Bargaining environment** | Information, outside options, time pressure |

No single factor dominates across all stages. The relative importance of these clusters varies between formation, governance, and termination.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Coalition Lifecycle** | Formation, governance, and termination are interconnected stages, not independent events |
| **Coalition Agreements** | Formal written agreements are key mechanisms for managing intra-coalition conflict |
| **Portfolio Allocation** | Cabinet distribution largely follows [Gamson's Law](../../papers/gamson-coalition-theory-resources/) but with significant deviations |
| **Conflict Management** | Coalitions develop specific institutional mechanisms (coalition committees, inner cabinets) to manage disagreements |
| **Electoral Consequences** | How a coalition performs affects its component parties' electoral fortunes, completing the lifecycle |

## The Coalition Lifecycle

| Stage | Key Dynamics | Primary Explanatory Factors |
|---|---|---|
| **Formation** | Bargaining over partnerships and portfolios | Institutions, preferences, bargaining environment |
| **Governance** | Policy implementation, conflict management | Coalition agreements, conflict resolution mechanisms |
| **Termination** | Strategic dissolution or coalition collapse | Critical events, electoral incentives, ideological tensions |
| **Electoral Aftermath** | Voters reward or punish coalition parties | Government performance, attribution of credit/blame |

## Open Questions

- **Does the lifecycle framework apply outside Western Europe?** Coalition politics in newer democracies, developing countries, and presidential systems may follow different patterns.
- **How has the lifecycle changed in the 21st century?** Rising populism, fragmented party systems, and social media may be disrupting traditional coalition dynamics.
- **Can the framework be extended to non-governmental coalitions?** International alliances and social movement coalitions also have lifecycles, though with different institutional structures.
