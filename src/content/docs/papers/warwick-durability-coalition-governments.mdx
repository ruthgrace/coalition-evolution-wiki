Paul Warwick's 1979 paper "The Durability of Coalition Governments in Parliamentary Democracies" was an early rigorous empirical study of coalition survival. His key finding was substantive: previous researchers had assumed that the risk of coalition termination was constant over a government's lifetime, but Warwick identified an important empirical pattern — termination rates actually *increase* over time, meaning governments become more fragile the longer they are in office.

This finding had major implications for how scholars model coalition dynamics. Warwick's identification of the **rising hazard rate** pattern — later formalized through event history analysis methods by scholars such as King, Alt, Burns, and Laver (1990) — opened up a new toolkit for studying when and why governments fall. His work also emphasized ideological diversity as a key driver of coalition fragility, contributing to the ongoing debate with Dodd's bargaining complexity hypothesis.

Warwick's empirical approach — grounded in large-N comparative data from West European parliamentary systems — set the standard for subsequent quantitative work on coalition duration. The debate between his "empiricist" school and the more formal-modeling approach of scholars like Laver and Shepsle became a defining methodological divide in the field.

## Background

Warwick undertook quantitative examination of government termination rates across West European parliamentary systems. His central finding was that the hazard rate — the probability of termination at any given point, conditional on having survived to that point — is not constant but rising.

This means coalitions don't just fail randomly. They accumulate stresses: policy disagreements compound, external shocks pile up, and electoral pressures intensify as the next election approaches.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Rising Hazard Rate** | The probability of coalition termination increases over the life of the government |
| **Ideological Diversity** | Coalitions spanning a wider ideological range are more fragile |
| **Empirical Focus** | Large-N comparative analysis of West European democracies |
| **Non-Constant Risk** | Rejects the assumption of constant termination probability used in earlier models |

## Comparative Findings

Warwick analyzed cabinet duration across multiple countries:

| Factor | Effect on Duration |
|---|---|
| Number of coalition parties | Negative (more parties = shorter) |
| Ideological range of coalition | Strongly negative |
| Majority status | Positive (majority coalitions last longer) |
| Economic conditions | Mixed effects depending on country and period |
| Presence of anti-system parties | Negative (reduces viable alternatives, but increases instability) |

## Open Questions

- **What is the causal mechanism behind rising hazard rates?** Is it accumulated policy disagreement, electoral cycle effects, or something else?
- **Do hazard rates rise in all institutional contexts?** Systems with fixed election dates may show different patterns than those where dissolution is at the government's discretion.
- **How do critical events interact with baseline hazard?** Later work by Browne, Frendreis, and Gleiber proposed an "events-based" model where exogenous shocks are the primary driver.
