Gregory Luebbert's *Liberalism, Fascism, or Social Democracy: Social Classes and the Political Origins of Regimes in Interwar Europe* (1991) is a landmark work in comparative historical analysis. Luebbert argued that the divergent political trajectories of Western European nations between the World Wars — some becoming stable liberal democracies, some social democracies, some fascist dictatorships — can be explained by the **class coalitions** that formed (or failed to form) during this critical period.

This is coalition theory applied to the biggest possible stakes: not who gets which ministry, but whether a country becomes a democracy or a dictatorship. Luebbert showed that the key variable was not economic development, culture, or institutional design, but the *political alliances between social classes* — particularly whether the urban middle class, the working class, and the peasantry could form stable governing coalitions.

The book was published posthumously (Luebbert died in 1988) and has become essential reading in comparative politics, historical sociology, and the study of democratic breakdown. Its framework remains relevant for understanding coalition dynamics in developing democracies today.

## Background

Luebbert's argument centers on three possible coalition configurations and their political outcomes:

| Coalition | Classes Involved | Political Outcome | Countries |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Liberal** | Unified middle class co-opts segments of working class | Liberal democracy | Britain, France, Switzerland |
| **Social Democratic** | Working class allies with family peasantry | Social democracy | Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Czechoslovakia |
| **Fascist** | Urban middle class allies with peasantry hostile to socialism | Fascism | Germany, Italy, Spain |

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Class Coalition as Cause** | The specific alliance of social classes determines regime type |
| **Middle Class Unity** | Where the middle class was unified and could co-opt workers, liberalism prevailed |
| **Red-Green Alliance** | Where social democrats allied with the peasantry, social democracy emerged |
| **Anti-Socialist Coalition** | Where the middle class and peasantry united against the working class, fascism resulted |
| **Pre-WWI Conditions Matter** | The political configurations established before 1914 set the constraints for interwar outcomes |

## The Critical Role of the Peasantry

A distinctive feature of Luebbert's analysis is the central role assigned to rural actors. In most coalition theory, the focus is on urban parties and parliamentary arithmetic. Luebbert showed that the peasantry was the **swing class** in interwar Europe:

- Allied with social democrats → social democracy (Scandinavia)
- Allied with the middle class against socialism → fascism (Germany, Italy)
- Marginalized by a strong liberal middle class → liberalism persists (Britain, France)

## Open Questions

- **Does the framework travel beyond interwar Europe?** Can Luebbert's class coalition logic explain regime outcomes in Latin America, Asia, or contemporary developing countries?
- **What about agency and contingency?** Luebbert's structural account leaves limited room for leadership decisions, political strategy, or contingent events.
- **How do Luebbert's coalitions relate to later party systems?** The class alliances he identifies are the deep structure beneath the party systems that coalition theorists like Riker and Axelrod study at the surface level.
