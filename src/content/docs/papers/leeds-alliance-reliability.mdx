Brett Ashley Leeds, Andrew Long, and Sara McLaughlin Mitchell's 2000 paper "Reevaluating Alliance Reliability" tackled a deceptively simple question: **how often do states actually honor their alliance commitments?** The answer — roughly **75% of the time** in their initial dataset, dropping to about **50%** with extended data — challenged both pessimists who viewed alliances as unreliable and optimists who took compliance for granted.

The key methodological contribution was the development of data that contributed to the **Alliance Treaty Obligations and Provisions (ATOP)** dataset, formally published in 2002 (Leeds, Ritter, Mitchell, and Long in *International Interactions*), which codes the specific provisions of alliance treaties rather than treating all alliances as equivalent. This matters because most alliances specify particular conditions under which obligations kick in and particular actions required. A state that doesn't intervene when an ally is attacked may be *honoring* its treaty if the treaty only covers defensive situations and the ally was the aggressor.

This work connects directly to [Snyder's (1984)](../../papers/snyder-alliance-security-dilemma/) theoretical framework on alliance reliability. Where Snyder analyzed the entrapment-abandonment dilemma theoretically, Leeds et al. provided the first systematic empirical evidence on how often abandonment actually occurs — and what predicts it.

## Background

Previous assessments of alliance reliability suffered from a coding problem: they treated all alliances as identical commitments and scored any non-intervention as a "failure." Leeds et al. showed that this dramatically overstated unreliability, because many instances of non-intervention occurred when the treaty's specific conditions had not been met.

## Key Findings

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Baseline reliability** | States honor military alliance commitments ~75% of the time (1816–1944 data) |
| **Extended data** | With data through 2003, overall compliance drops to ~50% |
| **Specificity matters** | Treaties with clearly defined obligations have higher compliance rates |
| **Regime type** | Democracies are more reliable alliance partners than non-democracies |
| **Power changes** | Shifts in relative power between allies predict treaty violation |

## Predictors of Alliance Violation

| Factor | Effect on Reliability |
|---|---|
| **Regime change** | Strongly negative — new governments may not honor predecessors' commitments |
| **Power shifts** | Negative — states whose power grows may see less need for the alliance |
| **Specificity of treaty** | Positive — clearer obligations are more likely to be honored |
| **Democratic regime** | Positive — audience costs and institutional constraints promote compliance |
| **Major power status** | Negative — great powers are more likely to violate (they have alternatives) |

## Open Questions

- **Does alliance reliability affect alliance formation?** If states know that commitments are honored only half the time, how does this shape which alliances form in the first place?
- **How do informal alliances compare?** The ATOP dataset focuses on formal treaties. Many important security relationships (e.g., US-Israel, US-Taiwan) are informal.
- **Is reliability endogenous to the alliance's value?** States may be more reliable when the alliance matters more to them — making "reliability" partly a measure of interest alignment rather than commitment credibility.
