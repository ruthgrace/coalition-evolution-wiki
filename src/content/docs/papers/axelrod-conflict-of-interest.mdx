Robert Axelrod's *Conflict of Interest: A Theory of Divergent Goals with Applications to Politics* (1970) introduced one of the most important refinements to coalition theory: the idea that ideology matters. Where Riker focused purely on size and winning, Axelrod argued that parties form coalitions with their ideological neighbors — producing what he called **connected coalitions** in which all members are adjacent on a policy dimension.

This was a fundamental break from size-based theories. Axelrod's model predicts that a center-right party would rather coalesce with its immediate right neighbor than with a distant left-wing party, even if the latter coalition were numerically smaller. The driving logic is conflict minimization: parties want to govern with partners whose policy preferences are close to their own.

The theory has performed well empirically, particularly in Western European parliamentary systems where parties can be meaningfully ordered on a left-right spectrum. Its main limitation is the assumption of a single policy dimension — real politics often involves multiple crosscutting cleavages.

## Background

Axelrod conceived of parties as occupying positions on a one-dimensional, ordinal policy space ranked from left to right. A **connected coalition** is one in which all constituent parties are adjacent in this space — no party is "skipped over." Among connected coalitions, Axelrod predicted the formation of **minimal connected winning coalitions (MCW)**: ideologically closed coalitions that would lose their majority if any member were excluded.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Connected Coalition** | All coalition members are adjacent on the ideological spectrum |
| **Minimal Connected Winning (MCW)** | The smallest connected coalition that holds a majority |
| **Conflict Minimization** | Parties seek to minimize ideological dispersion within the coalition |
| **Ordinal Policy Space** | Parties are ranked left-to-right; distances between them are not assumed to matter, only ordering |

## Comparison with Other Theories

| Theory | Prediction Basis | Key Variable |
|---|---|---|
| **Riker (1962)** | Size only | Number of seats |
| **Gamson (1961)** | Resource proportionality | Seat share |
| **Axelrod (1970)** | Ideological proximity | Policy adjacency |
| **De Swaan (1973)** | Policy distance | Cardinal distance on policy scale |

## Open Questions

- **What about multidimensional policy spaces?** If politics involves more than one dimension (e.g., economic left-right *and* social liberal-conservative), the notion of "connected" becomes ambiguous.
- **Do anti-system parties break the model?** Extremist parties that are adjacent on the spectrum but considered unacceptable coalition partners (e.g., far-right parties in many European systems) are often excluded in practice.
- **How does Axelrod's model interact with Gamson's Law?** Do ideologically connected coalitions also tend toward proportional payoff distribution?
