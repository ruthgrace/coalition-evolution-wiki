Michael Laver and Kenneth Shepsle's *Making and Breaking Governments* (1996) represents the most ambitious attempt to build a unified formal model of government formation and termination in parliamentary democracies. Their portfolio allocation model starts from a simple but powerful premise: the minister who controls a policy domain effectively *is* the policy in that domain. Coalitions form around credible allocations of ministerial portfolios, and they collapse when the balance of power shifts enough to make an alternative allocation viable.

The book integrates two strands of research that had previously been separate: coalition *formation* (who governs?) and coalition *termination* (when does the government fall?). By modeling both within a single framework, Laver and Shepsle showed that the same forces that bring a coalition together also contain the seeds of its eventual dissolution.

Their approach was tested against data from most postwar European parliamentary democracies, making it one of the few formal models in coalition research to receive systematic empirical evaluation. The results were mixed but productive, generating new hypotheses and sharpening debates about the relative importance of policy positions versus office-seeking motivations.

## Background

The **portfolio allocation model** rests on several key assumptions:

1. Policy is determined by the minister who holds the relevant portfolio
2. Parties have known policy positions in a multidimensional space
3. A government is a specific allocation of portfolios to parties
4. A government is "viable" only if no alternative allocation is preferred by a parliamentary majority

This framework turns government formation into a problem of finding *equilibrium* allocations — combinations of portfolio assignments that no majority can agree to overturn.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Ministerial Discretion** | The party that holds a ministry determines policy in that domain |
| **Portfolio Allocation as Equilibrium** | Governments form around stable assignments of ministries to parties |
| **Multidimensional Policy Space** | Parties have positions on multiple policy dimensions simultaneously |
| **Endogenous Termination** | Governments fall when shifts in party positions or seat shares make alternative allocations viable |

## Formation and Termination Linked

| Phase | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Formation** | Parties bargain over portfolio allocation; an allocation is viable if no majority prefers an alternative |
| **Maintenance** | Government persists as long as its portfolio allocation remains in equilibrium |
| **Termination** | Exogenous shocks (elections, party splits, policy crises) shift the landscape; a new majority coalesces around an alternative allocation |

## Open Questions

- **How realistic is ministerial discretion?** In practice, cabinet collective responsibility and coalition agreements constrain individual ministers. How much does this weaken the model?
- **Can the model handle more than two policy dimensions?** Equilibrium existence becomes more complex (and less guaranteed) as dimensionality increases.
- **What about the role of the formateur?** The model says relatively little about *who* gets to propose the initial allocation, which empirically matters a great deal.
