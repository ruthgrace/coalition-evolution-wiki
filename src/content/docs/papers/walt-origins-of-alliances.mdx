Stephen Walt's *The Origins of Alliances* (1987) introduced **balance of threat theory**, one of the most influential frameworks in international relations for understanding why states form alliances. Walt's key innovation was separating *power* from *threat*: states don't simply ally against the most powerful actor in the system, they ally against the most *threatening* one. Threat is a function of aggregate power, geographic proximity, offensive capabilities, and perceived aggressive intentions.

This refinement resolved a major puzzle in Cold War alliance behavior. The United States was more powerful than the Soviet Union for most of the Cold War, yet more states allied with Washington (through NATO) than with Moscow (through the Warsaw Pact). Balance of power theory predicted the opposite. Balance of threat theory explained why: the Soviet Union was perceived as more threatening due to its geographic position in Eurasia, its offensive military posture, and its ideological expansionism.

Walt also distinguished between two responses to threat: **balancing** (allying against the threat) and **bandwagoning** (allying with the threat). He argued that balancing is far more common, with bandwagoning largely limited to very weak states that have no other option.

## Background

Walt drew on extensive case studies from the Middle East (1955–1979) and the broader Cold War system to test his theory. His approach was explicitly comparative and empirical, distinguishing his work from purely theoretical treatments of alliance behavior.

The core claim is that alliance formation is driven by threat perception, which depends on four factors:

| Factor | Description |
|---|---|
| **Aggregate Power** | Total resources (population, industrial capacity, military spending) |
| **Geographic Proximity** | Nearby states pose greater threats than distant ones |
| **Offensive Capabilities** | States with force-projection ability are more threatening |
| **Perceived Intentions** | States believed to have aggressive goals trigger alliance responses |

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Balance of Threat** | States ally against threats, not just against power |
| **Balancing** | The dominant response: forming coalitions against the threatening state |
| **Bandwagoning** | Aligning with the threatening state; rare and mostly limited to weak states |
| **Ideology** | Shared ideology can facilitate alliance formation but is secondary to threat perception |

## Implications for Coalition Evolution

Walt's framework has direct implications for how coalitions of power shift over time:

- **Alliances dissolve when threats recede.** The end of the Cold War should have led to NATO's dissolution under balance-of-threat logic — yet NATO persisted and expanded. This remains a puzzle.
- **New threats generate new coalitions.** The rise of China has prompted new alliance structures (AUKUS, the Quad) consistent with Walt's predictions.
- **Internal alliance dynamics matter.** Threat perception is not uniform across alliance members, creating persistent tensions over burden-sharing and commitment.

## Open Questions

- **Why did NATO survive the end of the Cold War?** Institutional inertia, American hegemony, and the emergence of new threats (terrorism, Russia's resurgence) have all been proposed as explanations.
- **Can balance of threat theory explain non-military coalitions?** Economic blocs and diplomatic groupings may follow different logics.
- **How do domestic politics shape threat perception?** Walt's theory is primarily systemic; subsequent scholars have explored how domestic political coalitions influence foreign policy alignment.
