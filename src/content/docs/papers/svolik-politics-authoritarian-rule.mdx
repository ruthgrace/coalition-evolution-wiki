Milan Svolik's *The Politics of Authoritarian Rule* (2012) provides a comprehensive game-theoretic framework for understanding coalition dynamics within dictatorships. Svolik argues that authoritarian rulers face **two fundamental problems**: the problem of **authoritarian control** (keeping the masses in check) and the problem of **authoritarian power-sharing** (managing the ruling coalition of elites who could overthrow them).

The second problem — power-sharing — is where coalition theory becomes central. The dictator needs a coalition of military officers, party leaders, or other elites to govern effectively. But this coalition is inherently unstable: the dictator may try to accumulate more power at the coalition's expense, and coalition members may respond by staging a coup. The result is a strategic interaction shaped by credible threats, institutional constraints, and the ever-present possibility of violence.

Svolik's work complements [Acemoglu et al. (2008)](../../papers/acemoglu-coalition-formation-non-democracies/) by providing richer institutional detail and [Bueno de Mesquita et al. (2003)](../../papers/bueno-de-mesquita-logic-political-survival/) by focusing specifically on the dynamics *within* the ruling coalition rather than the coalition's relationship to the broader population.

## Background

Svolik's framework starts from a crucial structural feature of authoritarian politics: **no independent authority can enforce agreements among key actors**. In democracies, courts, constitutions, and elections constrain leaders. In dictatorships, violence is the ultimate arbiter. This means that power-sharing agreements within the ruling coalition are always fragile — they hold only as long as the balance of threat makes them self-enforcing.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Two Problems of Authoritarianism** | Control (over the masses) and power-sharing (among elites) |
| **Coup Threat as Constraint** | The ruling coalition deters dictatorial power grabs by threatening a coup |
| **Contested vs. Established Dictatorships** | Contested: ongoing power struggle within the coalition. Established: one actor has consolidated control |
| **Institutions as Commitment Devices** | Parties, legislatures, and constitutions in autocracies serve as mechanisms for credible power-sharing |

## Two Types of Authoritarian Equilibrium

| Type | Internal Dynamics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Contested Dictatorship** | Ongoing bargaining between dictator and coalition; coup threat is credible | Many military regimes; early years of new dictatorships |
| **Established Dictatorship** | Dictator has consolidated power; coalition members cannot credibly threaten a coup | Stalin after the purges; Mao after the Cultural Revolution |

The transition from contested to established dictatorship often involves the dynamics described by [Acemoglu et al.](../../papers/acemoglu-coalition-formation-non-democracies/): the dictator purges powerful coalition members until the remaining members are too weak to mount a challenge.

## Why Do Dictators Create Institutions?

A puzzle in authoritarian politics is why dictators bother with parties, legislatures, and elections. Svolik's answer: these institutions are **power-sharing mechanisms** that help solve the commitment problem within the ruling coalition.

| Institution | Function |
|---|---|
| **Regime Party** | Provides a forum for intra-elite bargaining; distributes patronage; socializes elites into the regime |
| **Legislature** | Allows coalition members to monitor the dictator's policy choices |
| **Controlled Elections** | Reveal information about popular support and opposition strength |
| **Military Council** | Formalizes power-sharing between the dictator and the armed forces |

## Open Questions

- **When do authoritarian power-sharing institutions actually work?** China's Communist Party has managed relatively smooth leadership transitions; many other single-party states have not. What explains the variation?
- **How do external pressures (sanctions, international norms) interact with internal coalition dynamics?** The framework is primarily domestic-focused.
- **Can Svolik's framework predict regime transitions?** The theory explains stability and instability within authoritarian rule but is less clear about when authoritarian coalitions collapse entirely.
