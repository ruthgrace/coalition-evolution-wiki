Nella Van Dyke and Brian Amos's "Social Movement Coalitions: Formation, Longevity, and Success" (2017) provides a comprehensive survey of research on coalitions among social movements — a domain where coalition dynamics look quite different from parliamentary or international settings. Movement coalitions are typically informal, lack binding agreements, cross organizational and identity boundaries, and face distinctive challenges around power, resources, and trust.

The paper synthesizes decades of research on antiwar, environmental, labor, ethnic, and women's movements to identify the key factors that determine whether movement coalitions form, how long they last, and whether they achieve their goals. A central finding is that **tolerance for difference** and **negotiation capacity** are the strongest predictors of coalition longevity and success.

This literature bridges political science and sociology, connecting formal coalition theory with the lived experience of organizers trying to hold together diverse alliances. It also highlights the particular difficulties of **cross-class coalitions**, where power asymmetries and cultural differences between participants can undermine solidarity.

## Background

Social movement coalitions differ from parliamentary coalitions in several key ways:

| Dimension | Parliamentary Coalitions | Social Movement Coalitions |
|---|---|---|
| **Formality** | Formal agreements, portfolio allocation | Informal agreements, shared campaigns |
| **Binding Commitments** | Government formation, legislative votes | Voluntary coordination, joint actions |
| **Members** | Political parties with defined seat shares | Organizations, networks, individuals with varying resources |
| **Success Metric** | Government formation / survival | Policy change, public opinion shifts, mobilization |
| **Exit Costs** | Government collapse | Organizational withdrawal, reduced mobilization |

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Tolerance for Difference** | Coalitions that can manage internal disagreement last longer |
| **Bridge Builders** | Individuals who can translate between different movement cultures are essential |
| **Shared Threat** | External threats (policy threats, repression) are the strongest driver of coalition formation |
| **Resource Asymmetry** | When well-resourced organizations dominate, less-resourced partners may exit |
| **Collective Identity** | Organizations with strong collective identity are better able to negotiate within coalitions without losing their constituency |

## Cross-Class Coalition Challenges

A major theme in the literature is the difficulty of building coalitions across class lines:

- Cultural differences between middle-class professionals and working-class or low-income participants create friction
- Ideological congruence is less likely when there is greater social distance between participants
- Middle- and high-income activists may inadvertently dominate agenda-setting
- Organizations with low-income constituencies need strong internal accountability to avoid being co-opted

## Open Questions

- **What makes cross-movement coalitions durable?** Most movement coalitions are short-lived and campaign-specific. What distinguishes the ones that persist?
- **How do digital platforms change coalition dynamics?** Online organizing may lower the costs of coalition formation but also reduce the depth of commitment.
- **Can insights from movement coalitions inform parliamentary coalition theory (and vice versa)?** The two literatures have developed largely in isolation.
