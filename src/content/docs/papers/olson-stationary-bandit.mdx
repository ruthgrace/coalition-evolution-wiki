Mancur Olson's 1993 article "Dictatorship, Democracy, and Development" introduced the **stationary bandit** metaphor, one of the most elegant frameworks in political economy for understanding how power coalitions relate to governance quality. The argument starts from anarchy: without any organized power, "roving bandits" engage in uncoordinated theft, destroying all incentive to invest or produce.

A bandit who settles in one place and monopolizes theft — becoming a **stationary bandit** — gains an "encompassing interest" in the territory's prosperity. Unlike a roving bandit who takes everything and moves on, the stationary bandit wants the economy to grow because a richer territory means more to tax. This logic leads the autocrat to provide public goods (protection, infrastructure, contract enforcement) and to tax at less than 100% — not out of benevolence, but out of self-interest.

The framework has profound implications for coalition dynamics. It explains why even the most extractive power holders have incentives to govern, why democracies (where the ruling coalition encompasses the entire population) tend to provide better governance than autocracies, and why transitions from anarchy to autocracy to democracy can be understood as expansions of the ruling coalition's "encompassing interest."

## Background

Olson's argument proceeds through three stages:

| Stage | Power Structure | Governing Logic |
|---|---|---|
| **Anarchy** | Roving bandits — no monopoly on force | Total extraction; no investment; economic collapse |
| **Autocracy** | Stationary bandit — monopoly on force | Partial extraction; some public goods; encompassing interest |
| **Democracy** | Ruling coalition encompasses the majority | Broad public goods provision; lower extraction rates |

The key insight is that the transition from roving to stationary bandit is driven by self-interest, not ideology. A bandit who can monopolize theft in a territory is better off settling down, reducing the tax rate, and investing in the territory's productivity.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Roving Bandit** | Uncoordinated extraction under anarchy; destroys economic incentives |
| **Stationary Bandit** | Monopolist of theft who gains an interest in the territory's prosperity |
| **Encompassing Interest** | The larger the share of society's output that accrues to the ruler, the more the ruler benefits from economic growth |
| **Autocrat as Tax Optimizer** | The rational autocrat sets taxes below 100% to maximize long-run revenue |

## Open Questions

- **When do stationary bandits become democratic?** The theory explains why autocracy is better than anarchy but is less clear about what drives the transition to democracy.
- **Can the model explain predatory states?** Some autocrats appear to behave more like roving bandits than stationary ones, extracting resources without investing in productivity. What determines which type of autocrat emerges?
- **How do coalitions within the ruling elite affect the stationary bandit's behavior?** Olson treats the autocrat as a unitary actor, but [Svolik (2012)](../../papers/svolik-politics-authoritarian-rule/) and [Acemoglu et al. (2008)](../../papers/acemoglu-coalition-formation-non-democracies/) show that intra-elite coalitions significantly constrain dictators.
