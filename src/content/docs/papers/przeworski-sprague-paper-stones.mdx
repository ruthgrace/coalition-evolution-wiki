Adam Przeworski and John Sprague's *Paper Stones: A History of Electoral Socialism* (1986) identified a fundamental **coalition dilemma** facing social democratic parties: the working class alone is rarely large enough to win elections, so social democrats must appeal to middle-class voters — but broadening the coalition dilutes the party's class identity and risks alienating its working-class base.

The title refers to the socialist hope that **ballots ("paper stones") could replace barricades** — that socialism could be achieved through electoral competition rather than revolution. But Przeworski and Sprague showed that this electoral strategy contains an inherent structural tension. Every step toward the middle class is a step away from the working class, and the net electoral effect is uncertain.

This is coalition theory applied to the **mass electoral level**: not which parties form a government, but which social groups form a party's electoral coalition. The dilemma Przeworski and Sprague identified continues to shape left-wing politics worldwide, from European social democrats losing working-class voters to populist right parties, to American Democrats navigating the tension between their college-educated and working-class constituencies.

## Background

The dilemma arises from a structural feature of class-based politics in democracies:

| Condition | Implication |
|---|---|
| Workers are a minority of the electorate | Social democrats cannot win with workers alone |
| Appealing to the middle class dilutes class identity | Workers may defect to other parties or abstain |
| Not appealing to the middle class guarantees defeat | Permanent minority status |

German Social Democrats recognized this as early as the late 19th century, when some leaders advocated extending socialist appeals to the petite bourgeoisie, peasants, and white-collar employees.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Electoral Dilemma** | Broadening the coalition is necessary to win but risks losing the base |
| **Class Dealignment** | As parties broaden their appeal, class voting weakens and electoral coalitions become more heterogeneous |
| **Trade Union Role** | Strong unions help maintain working-class loyalty even as the party courts middle-class voters |
| **Structural Constraint** | The dilemma is not a strategic failure but a structural feature of electoral socialism in capitalist democracies |

## The Dilemma in Practice

| Strategy | Gains | Losses |
|---|---|---|
| **Stay pure (workers only)** | Strong class identity, loyal base | Permanent electoral minority |
| **Broaden to middle class** | Potential electoral majority | Diluted identity, working-class defection |
| **"Third Way" synthesis** | Short-term electoral success | Long-term erosion of class base (as seen in 2000s–2010s) |

## Connection to Other Coalition Theories

Przeworski and Sprague's dilemma connects to several other frameworks:

- [Luebbert (1991)](/papers/luebbert-liberalism-fascism-social-democracy/): The "red-green" alliance between workers and peasants was one historical solution to the electoral dilemma
- [Moore (1966)](/papers/moore-social-origins/): The strength of the bourgeoisie determines whether social democrats face a viable coalition partner or an implacable opponent
- [Key (1955)](/papers/key-critical-elections/): Critical realignments can suddenly resolve or re-create the dilemma by reshuffling which groups are available as coalition partners

## Open Questions

- **Is the dilemma still relevant in post-industrial societies?** The traditional working class has shrunk dramatically. Is the new dilemma about education polarization rather than class?
- **Can left parties solve the dilemma through issue diversification?** Emphasizing non-economic issues (environment, identity, governance) may attract new voters without directly alienating the base.
- **How has the rise of populist right parties changed the calculus?** Working-class voters defecting to right-wing populists represents the dilemma's most acute contemporary manifestation.
