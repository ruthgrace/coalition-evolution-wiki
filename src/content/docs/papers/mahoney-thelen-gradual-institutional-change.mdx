James Mahoney and Kathleen Thelen's *Explaining Institutional Change* (2010) addressed a gap in political science: most theories explained institutional *stability* or *revolutionary change*, but not the **gradual, incremental transformation** that characterizes most real-world institutional evolution. Their framework identifies four modes of gradual change — **displacement, layering, drift, and conversion** — each driven by different coalition dynamics and political contexts.

The framework is deeply rooted in coalition theory. Mahoney and Thelen see institutions as **distributional instruments** that emerge from and are shaped by power-laden, coalition-based conflicts over scarce resources. Institutional change happens when the coalition supporting the status quo weakens, when challengers find creative ways to work around existing rules, or when the environment shifts in ways that alter what existing rules actually produce.

This connects the study of institutional change to the coalition dynamics literature by showing that institutions don't just reflect the interests of the coalition that created them — they are **continuously contested** by coalitions that seek to reshape them.

## Background

Traditional institutionalism emphasized stability: institutions create "path dependence" that locks in particular arrangements. But Mahoney and Thelen observed that many of the most consequential institutional changes happen gradually, without a clear "critical juncture." Their framework explains how.

## Four Modes of Gradual Change

| Mode | Mechanism | Coalition Dynamic | Political Context |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Displacement** | Old rules replaced by new ones | Challengers strong enough to impose new rules | Weak veto players |
| **Layering** | New rules added alongside old ones | Challengers can't change old rules but can add new ones | Strong veto players but open to amendments |
| **Drift** | Existing rules not updated as environment changes | Defenders of status quo block adaptation; neglect erodes effectiveness | Veto players block maintenance |
| **Conversion** | Existing rules reinterpreted for new purposes | Actors exploit ambiguity in rules to serve different goals | Rules are ambiguous; enforcement is contested |

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Institutions as Distributional Instruments** | Institutions allocate resources and power among competing groups |
| **Continuous Contestation** | Even "stable" institutions are constantly being pushed and pulled by coalitions |
| **Change Agents** | Different types of change are driven by different types of actors (insurrectionaries, symbionts, subversives, opportunists) |
| **Context Determines Mode** | The strength of veto players and the degree of discretion in enforcement determine which mode of change is possible |

## Examples

| Mode | Example |
|---|---|
| **Displacement** | Welfare state reforms that replace universal programs with means-tested ones |
| **Layering** | The gradual expansion of the EU through treaty amendments added alongside the original framework |
| **Drift** | The erosion of US labor protections as enforcement weakened without formal legal change |
| **Conversion** | China's township and village enterprises: socialist collective institutions repurposed for market capitalism |

## Connection to Coalition Theory

The framework has direct implications for understanding how coalitions of power change over time:

- **Coalitions don't always need to win outright** to reshape the institutional landscape. Layering, drift, and conversion allow weaker coalitions to achieve gradual change.
- **Institutional change can shift coalition power.** A small institutional tweak (layering) may, over time, create new interests and new coalition possibilities.
- **The mode of change reflects the balance of power.** Displacement happens when challengers are strong; layering and conversion happen when they're weaker but creative.

## Open Questions

- **How do the four modes interact?** Real institutional change may involve multiple modes operating simultaneously or sequentially.
- **Can the framework predict which mode of change will occur?** The theory is good at classifying observed changes but less clear about predicting future trajectories.
- **How does this connect to the [Advocacy Coalition Framework](../../papers/sabatier-advocacy-coalition-framework/)?** Sabatier's ACF focuses on policy change through belief-based coalitions; Mahoney and Thelen focus on institutional change through distributional coalitions. The two frameworks may be complementary.
