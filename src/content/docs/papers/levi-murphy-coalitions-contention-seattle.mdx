Margaret Levi and Gillian Murphy's 2006 paper "Coalitions of Contention: The Case of the WTO Protests in Seattle" analyzed one of the most dramatic examples of social movement coalition-building in recent history: the broad alliance of labor unions, environmental groups, consumer advocates, development NGOs, student organizations, and anarchist groups that converged on Seattle in November 1999 to protest the World Trade Organization.

The "Battle of Seattle" became iconic for the sheer diversity of its coalition — captured in the phrase **"teamsters and turtles"** (trade unionists and environmentalists). Levi and Murphy used the case to investigate what makes such cross-movement coalitions possible and what limits their durability.

Their central finding: shared interests and compatible framing are **necessary but not sufficient** for coalition formation. Coalition partners must also calculate a benefit from pooling resources and be able to **credibly commit** to delivering on promises and managing internal tensions. This emphasis on credible commitment connects social movement coalition theory to the formal bargaining literature in ways that [Van Dyke and Amos (2017)](../../papers/van-dyke-amos-social-movement-coalitions/) later developed further.

## Background

The 1999 WTO protests brought together organizations with very different constituencies, ideologies, and tactical preferences:

| Coalition Participant | Primary Concern | Tactics |
|---|---|---|
| AFL-CIO and affiliated unions | Labor standards in trade agreements | Permitted marches, lobbying |
| Environmental groups (Sierra Club, etc.) | Environmental provisions in trade rules | Permitted protests, media campaigns |
| Global Exchange and development NGOs | Impact of trade liberalization on developing countries | Direct action, teach-ins |
| Student groups | Sweatshop labor, corporate power | Direct action, sit-ins |
| Anarchist groups | Anti-capitalism, anti-globalization | Property destruction, confrontation |

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Necessary but Not Sufficient** | Shared interests and compatible framing alone don't produce coalitions; credible commitment matters |
| **Resource Pooling** | Partners must calculate a concrete benefit from combining resources |
| **Credible Commitment** | Each partner must believe others will deliver promised resources and manage internal tensions |
| **Coalition Fragility** | Tactical disagreements (especially over property destruction) created persistent tensions |
| **Event Coalitions** | Short-lived coalitions organized around a specific protest event rather than ongoing collaboration |

## Why the Coalition Formed — and Why It Was Fragile

| Factor | Enabled Coalition Formation | Threatened Coalition Durability |
|---|---|---|
| Common target (WTO) | Yes — diverse groups could agree on what they opposed | No — disagreement on what they wanted *instead* |
| Shared moral commitments | Yes — broad consensus on fairness and accountability | Partially — different moral frameworks (labor rights vs. environmentalism vs. anti-capitalism) |
| Resource complementarity | Yes — unions had money and members; NGOs had expertise and media access | No — tactical disagreements over how to deploy resources |
| Credible commitment | Partially — formal agreements between some organizations | Weak — anarchist groups could not be bound by agreements |

## Open Questions

- **Can event coalitions become durable movements?** The Seattle coalition largely dispersed after the protests. What would it take to sustain such broad alliances?
- **How do tactical disagreements (violence vs. nonviolence) affect coalition viability?** The property destruction by some anarchist groups created a wedge within the broader coalition.
- **Has the model of coalition protest changed since 1999?** Social media, decentralized organizing, and the rise of movements like Occupy and climate strikes suggest new coalition dynamics.
