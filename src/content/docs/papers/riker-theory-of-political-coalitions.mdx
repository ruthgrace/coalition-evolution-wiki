William H. Riker's *The Theory of Political Coalitions* (1962) is widely regarded as the founding text of formal coalition theory in political science. By applying n-person game theory to politics, Riker argued that rational actors will form coalitions that are just large enough to win — and no larger. This "size principle" launched decades of research, debate, and refinement.

The book marked a turning point in how political scientists studied power. Before Riker, coalition analysis was largely descriptive. After him, it became a domain of formal models, testable hypotheses, and sustained empirical investigation. The minimum winning coalition hypothesis remains a benchmark against which virtually all subsequent coalition theories are measured.

Despite its elegance, the theory has faced significant criticism. Real-world coalitions frequently exceed minimum winning size, and the zero-sum assumption underlying the model rarely holds in actual parliamentary politics. Yet the framework's influence is undeniable — it defined the questions the field would spend the next half-century trying to answer.

## Background

Riker drew on the theory of n-person zero-sum games to derive his central claim:

> "In n-person, zero-sum games, where side-payments are permitted, where players are rational, and where they have perfect information, only minimum winning coalitions occur."

The logic is straightforward: in a zero-sum world, any surplus coalition members dilute the payoff for everyone else. Rational actors therefore have an incentive to exclude unnecessary partners.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Size Principle** | Coalitions will tend toward the minimum size needed to win |
| **Zero-Sum Assumption** | Politics is modeled as a fixed-pie contest over power |
| **Rational Actor Model** | Players seek to maximize their share of the payoff |
| **Information Conditions** | Perfect information leads to minimum winning; imperfect information may lead to larger coalitions as a hedge against uncertainty |

Riker also introduced the idea that coalition politics could be studied with the same rigor as economic behavior, borrowing tools from von Neumann and Morgenstern's game theory.

## Open Questions

- **Why are oversized coalitions so common?** Empirically, many parliamentary coalitions include more parties than strictly necessary. Subsequent scholars (Axelrod, De Swaan, Dodd) proposed alternative explanations rooted in ideology, policy distance, and institutional incentives.
- **Is politics really zero-sum?** If coalition partners can expand the total payoff through policy compromise, the size principle's predictions break down.
- **How does uncertainty change things?** Riker acknowledged that imperfect information could lead to larger coalitions, but the formal treatment of uncertainty came later.
