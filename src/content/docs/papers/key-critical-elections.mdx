V.O. Key Jr.'s 1955 article "A Theory of Critical Elections" introduced one of the most influential concepts in American political science: the idea that certain elections represent fundamental, lasting realignments of party coalitions. Rather than treating all elections as interchangeable, Key argued that some elections are **critical** — they produce sharp, durable shifts in which social groups, regions, and interests align with which party.

The concept reshaped how scholars think about coalition evolution in democracies. Instead of gradual, continuous change, Key's framework suggests that party coalitions are punctuated equilibria: long periods of stability interrupted by sudden, dramatic reorganization. The classic examples — 1860 (Civil War realignment), 1896 (industrialization realignment), 1932 (New Deal realignment) — each produced party coalitions that persisted for a generation or more.

Walter Dean Burnham later extended Key's framework in *Critical Elections and the Mainsprings of American Politics* (1970), arguing that realignments occur on a roughly 30-36 year cycle driven by the inability of existing party coalitions to address emerging crises. David Mayhew's *Electoral Realignments* (2002) offered a major critique, arguing the evidence for periodic sharp realignments is weaker than commonly claimed and that coalition change is often more gradual and multidimensional.

## Background

Key's original analysis focused on New England towns where he could identify sharp, persistent shifts in voting patterns. His method was straightforward: compare election returns across time and look for elections where the pattern breaks decisively and the new pattern endures.

The theory rests on a distinction between three types of elections:

| Election Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Maintaining** | The existing party coalition holds; the majority party wins |
| **Deviating** | The minority party wins, but the underlying coalition structure is unchanged |
| **Critical/Realigning** | The coalitional bases of the parties shift durably; a new majority coalition forms |

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Critical Elections** | Elections that produce sharp, lasting changes in party coalitions |
| **Durability** | The new alignment persists across multiple subsequent elections |
| **Social Group Realignment** | Entire demographic or regional blocs shift their partisan loyalty |
| **Punctuated Change** | Coalition evolution is not gradual but occurs in sudden bursts |

## Major American Realignments

| Year | Trigger | Coalition Shift |
|---|---|---|
| **1860** | Slavery and Civil War | South becomes solidly Democratic; North becomes Republican |
| **1896** | Industrialization, agrarian populism | Urban industrial interests consolidate behind Republicans; Democrats become agrarian/Southern party |
| **1932** | Great Depression | FDR's New Deal coalition: labor, urban ethnic groups, African Americans, the South |
| **1960s–80s** | Civil rights, cultural issues | Southern whites shift to Republicans; college-educated suburbanites begin shifting to Democrats |

## Open Questions

- **Is realignment theory still useful?** Mayhew (2002) argued the evidence for periodic, sharp realignments is weaker than claimed. Coalition change may be more continuous than punctuated.
- **Are we in a realignment now?** The ongoing reshuffling of American party coalitions along education and urban/rural lines has prompted debate about whether a new critical realignment is underway.
- **Does the framework apply outside the US?** Party systems in proportional representation democracies may evolve differently, with new parties emerging rather than existing parties' coalitions reshuffling.
