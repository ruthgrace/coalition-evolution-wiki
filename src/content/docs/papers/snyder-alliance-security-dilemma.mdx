Glenn Snyder's 1984 article "The Security Dilemma in Alliance Politics" and his later book *Alliance Politics* (1997) developed one of the most influential frameworks for understanding the *internal* dynamics of coalitions in international relations. While Walt's balance of threat theory explains why alliances form, Snyder explains what happens *inside* them: the constant tension between **entrapment** (being dragged into a conflict by an ally) and **abandonment** (being left exposed when an ally defects).

Snyder's insight was that alliances are not static commitments but ongoing bargaining relationships. Each member must constantly calibrate its level of commitment: too strong a commitment risks entrapment; too weak a commitment risks abandonment. This dilemma is structurally similar to the classic security dilemma between adversaries, but applied to relations between partners.

The framework integrates neorealism, bargaining theory, game theory, and coalition formation theory, and was tested against detailed case studies of European alliance politics from 1879 to 1914 — the era of shifting great-power coalitions that preceded World War I.

## Background

Snyder's alliance security dilemma involves a fundamental choice: **support or nonsupport** of an ally in a crisis. The tension arises because:

- **Strong support** reassures the ally but risks being drawn into their conflicts (entrapment)
- **Weak support** preserves autonomy but risks the ally seeking other partners or accommodating the adversary (abandonment)

The severity of the dilemma depends on system structure. In **multipolar** systems, where allies have alternatives, the fear of abandonment is high and entrapment risk is managed by the availability of realignment. In **bipolar** systems, where alternatives are limited, abandonment is less likely but entrapment is a greater concern.

## Key Arguments

| Concept | Description |
|---|---|
| **Entrapment** | Being drawn into a conflict by an ally's actions or commitments |
| **Abandonment** | An ally defecting, realigning, or failing to provide support when needed |
| **Alliance Bargaining** | Allies continuously negotiate the terms of their commitment |
| **Parallel to Adversary Dilemma** | The entrapment-abandonment tradeoff mirrors the arms race-security spiral tradeoff between enemies |

## Multipolar vs. Bipolar Dynamics

| System Type | Abandonment Risk | Entrapment Risk | Alliance Flexibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Multipolar** | High (allies have alternatives) | Lower (can realign) | High |
| **Bipolar** | Low (no alternatives) | Higher (locked in) | Low |

Snyder used this framework to analyze twenty-two episodes of intra-alliance bargaining in the pre-WWI European system, showing how shifts in alliance commitments contributed to the escalation dynamics that led to war.

## Open Questions

- **How does the entrapment-abandonment dilemma play out in modern alliance structures?** NATO's Article 5 commitments, for instance, create strong anti-abandonment guarantees but raise entrapment concerns for smaller members.
- **Can the framework apply to non-military coalitions?** Trade blocs, political parties, and social movement coalitions all involve analogous commitment dilemmas.
- **How do domestic politics interact with alliance bargaining?** Leaders may face domestic pressure to either strengthen or weaken alliance commitments, complicating the strategic calculus.
