---
title: Coalition Duration
description: What makes coalitions last — and what causes them to fall apart.
---

Once a coalition forms, the clock starts ticking. Some coalitions endure for decades (NATO has lasted over 75 years); others collapse within months. Understanding what determines coalition **durability** is one of the most empirically rich areas of coalition research, spanning parliamentary governments, international alliances, and social movements.

The central debate in the parliamentary literature is between two rival explanations: the [bargaining complexity hypothesis](/terminology/bargaining-complexity-hypothesis/) (more partners = shorter duration) and the [ideological diversity hypothesis](/terminology/ideological-diversity-hypothesis/) (wider ideological range = shorter duration). But the research on alliance persistence and social movement coalitions adds additional dimensions: institutional inertia, shared identity, external threat, and the organizational capacity to manage internal disagreement.

## Parliamentary Coalition Survival

[Dodd (1976)](/papers/dodd-coalitions-parliamentary-government/) made the foundational argument that **minimum winning coalitions tend to last longest**. As coalitions depart from minimum winning status — becoming either undersized minorities or oversized surplus coalitions — durability decreases. The logic: more parties in government means more complex bargaining, more veto players, and more potential for disagreement.

[Warwick (1979)](/papers/warwick-durability-coalition-governments/) introduced a key methodological and substantive finding: coalition termination risk is **not constant over time but rising**. Governments become more fragile the longer they are in office, as policy disagreements accumulate, electoral pressures intensify, and external shocks pile up. This **rising hazard rate** finding transformed how scholars model coalition survival.

| Factor | Effect on Duration | Primary Source |
|---|---|---|
| Number of coalition parties | Negative | Dodd (1976) |
| Ideological range | Strongly negative | Warwick (1979) |
| Majority status | Positive | Dodd (1976), Warwick (1979) |
| Rising hazard over time | Increases termination probability | Warwick (1979) |

[Laver and Shepsle (1996)](/papers/laver-shepsle-making-breaking-governments/) unified formation and termination in a single framework: coalitions persist as long as their portfolio allocation remains in equilibrium, and collapse when shifts in party positions or seat shares make an alternative allocation viable to a parliamentary majority. This treats termination not as random breakdown but as a structural consequence of changing political conditions.

## Alliance Persistence: The NATO Puzzle

The [NATO/Warsaw Pact comparison](/papers/nato-warsaw-pact-alliance-dissolution/) is the most dramatic case study in coalition duration. When the Cold War ended, both alliances lost their founding rationale. The Warsaw Pact dissolved within two years. NATO not only survived but expanded from 16 to 32 members.

The leading explanations draw on multiple coalition theories:

| Explanation | Why NATO Lasted | Why the Warsaw Pact Didn't |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional inertia | Deep bureaucratic integration, habits of cooperation | Held together by Soviet coercion, not genuine institutions |
| Member satisfaction | Members benefited from the US-led order | Members had been coerced into joining |
| New threats | Balkans instability, terrorism, Russia's resurgence | No new purpose after Soviet collapse |
| Democratic norms | Shared values provided a non-military rationale | No shared normative foundation |

The case shows that coalition duration depends not just on the original threat or interest that brought members together, but on the **institutional and normative infrastructure** built during the coalition's lifetime.

## Social Movement Coalitions: Typically Short-Lived

[Van Dyke and Amos (2017)](/papers/van-dyke-amos-social-movement-coalitions/) document that social movement coalitions are typically **episodic** — forming around specific campaigns or threats and dissolving when the immediate impetus passes. The factors that predict longevity are:

- **Tolerance for difference**: coalitions that can manage internal disagreement last longer
- **Bridge builders**: individuals who translate between different movement cultures
- **Organizational accountability**: groups with strong internal governance can sustain coalition commitments without losing their constituency
- **Sustained external threat**: ongoing (not just episodic) threats keep coalitions together

The contrast with parliamentary and alliance coalitions is instructive. Movement coalitions lack the institutional scaffolding (coalition agreements, portfolio allocations, treaty commitments) that stabilize formal coalitions. Their durability depends almost entirely on relational capacity and sustained motivation.

## Cross-Domain Patterns

Across all three domains, several factors consistently predict coalition duration:

1. **Internal cohesion matters more than size.** Ideologically narrow coalitions (Warwick), satisfied alliance members (NATO), and movements with bridge-builders (Van Dyke & Amos) all outlast their more diverse or coerced counterparts.
2. **Institutions stabilize.** Formal agreements, integrated command structures, and shared bureaucracies create path dependence that extends coalition life beyond what pure interest-based calculations would predict.
3. **External threats are a double-edged sword.** Threats bring coalitions together but also make them dependent on the threat's continuation. When the threat disappears, coalitions without other binding mechanisms dissolve (Warsaw Pact).
