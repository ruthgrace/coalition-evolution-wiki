---
title: Bargaining and Getting Things Done
description: How coalition members negotiate, divide spoils, manage conflict, and actually accomplish their goals.
---

Forming a coalition is only the beginning. Once a coalition exists, its members must **bargain** over how to divide the benefits, **manage** internal disagreements, and **coordinate** action toward shared goals — all while each member retains their own interests and exit options. This section covers the research on what happens *inside* coalitions: how deals get made, how payoffs are distributed, and how partners navigate the constant tension between cooperation and self-interest.

The literature here spans legislative politics, international alliances, and policy advocacy — very different settings that turn out to share surprisingly similar bargaining dynamics.

## Legislative Bargaining: The Proposer's Advantage

[Baron and Ferejohn (1989)](/papers/baron-ferejohn-bargaining-legislatures/) built the foundational model of how legislative coalitions actually form through explicit bargaining. Their key insight: **whoever gets to propose the deal captures a disproportionate share of the payoff**. In their model, a randomly selected proposer offers a division of resources, and a majority must accept or reject it. Because delay is costly (future payoffs are discounted), voters accept deals that give them less than their "fair" share — they'd rather take a bad deal now than risk getting nothing in the next round.

This **proposer advantage** explains a real-world pattern: the party that leads coalition negotiations (the formateur) often secures more cabinet portfolios than its seat share alone would predict, exceeding the proportional distribution that [Gamson's Law](/papers/gamson-coalition-theory-resources/) otherwise predicts.

## Dividing the Spoils: Gamson's Law

[Gamson (1961)](/papers/gamson-coalition-theory-resources/) established the most robust empirical regularity in coalition studies: payoffs tend to be distributed **in proportion to the resources each member contributes**. In parliamentary coalitions, this means parties receive cabinet seats roughly proportional to the legislative seats they bring to the coalition.

| Prediction | Source | When It Holds |
|---|---|---|
| Strict proportionality | Gamson's Law | Most parliamentary coalitions |
| Formateur bonus (above proportional) | Baron & Ferejohn | When one party leads negotiations |
| Small party bonus | Empirical finding | When small parties are pivotal |

The tension between Gamson's proportionality norm and Baron-Ferejohn's proposer advantage remains an active area of research. Both patterns appear in the data, and the question is when each dominates.

## Alliance Bargaining: Entrapment vs. Abandonment

[Snyder (1984)](/papers/snyder-alliance-security-dilemma/) showed that bargaining within international alliances is structured by a fundamental dilemma: each member must calibrate its commitment level, balancing the risk of **entrapment** (being dragged into an ally's conflict) against the risk of **abandonment** (the ally defecting when you need them).

This dilemma creates ongoing negotiation within alliances:

| Action | Reduces Risk Of | Increases Risk Of |
|---|---|---|
| Stronger commitment to ally | Abandonment | Entrapment |
| Weaker commitment to ally | Entrapment | Abandonment |

The dynamics play out differently across system types. In **multipolar** systems, allies have alternatives, so abandonment fears dominate. In **bipolar** systems, alternatives are scarce, so entrapment is the greater concern. Either way, alliance management is a continuous bargaining process, not a one-time agreement.

[Leeds et al. (2000)](/papers/leeds-alliance-reliability/) provided the first systematic empirical evidence on this bargaining dynamic, finding that states honor their alliance commitments roughly **75% of the time** — but that reliability varies significantly based on treaty specificity, regime type, and shifts in relative power. Democracies are more reliable partners, and treaties with clearly defined obligations see higher compliance. The findings confirm that alliance bargaining is shaped not just by the entrapment-abandonment dilemma but by the credibility of commitments.

## Policy Coalitions: The Long Game

[Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith's Advocacy Coalition Framework (1993)](/papers/sabatier-advocacy-coalition-framework/) shifts the lens from legislative and military coalitions to **policy subsystems** — the networks of politicians, bureaucrats, interest groups, and researchers who compete to shape policy in a given domain. Getting things done in this context means not just passing a single bill but sustaining coordinated action over a decade or more.

The ACF identifies three mechanisms through which advocacy coalitions accomplish (or fail to accomplish) their goals:

| Mechanism | Speed | What Changes |
|---|---|---|
| **Policy-oriented learning** | Slow (years) | Evidence gradually shifts instrumental strategies |
| **External shocks** | Fast | Crises redistribute attention and resources |
| **Negotiated agreements** | Medium | Opposing coalitions find compromise on specific points |

The key insight is that deep beliefs rarely change, so coalition membership is sticky. Progress comes through incremental learning, strategic compromise, and capitalizing on exogenous events — not through persuading opponents to switch sides.

## The Full Coalition Lifecycle

[Strom, Muller, and Bergman (2008)](/papers/strom-muller-bergman-cabinets-coalition-bargaining/) synthesized decades of research into a unified framework covering the **full coalition lifecycle** — formation, governance, and termination — across all Western European parliamentary democracies in the post-1945 period. Their central finding is that these stages are deeply interconnected: how a coalition forms shapes how it governs, which shapes when and how it ends. Coalitions develop specific institutional mechanisms to manage internal bargaining — coalition agreements, coalition committees, and inner cabinets — that go well beyond the initial portfolio allocation. The lifecycle framework integrates the proposer advantage (Baron & Ferejohn), proportional payoff norms (Gamson), and duration dynamics into a single empirical picture.
