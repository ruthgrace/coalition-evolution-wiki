---
title: Authoritarian Governments
description: How coalitions of power form, shift, and collapse in non-democratic regimes.
---

Coalition dynamics don't stop at the borders of democracy. Authoritarian regimes are also built on coalitions — of military factions, party elites, ethnic groups, economic oligarchs, and social classes. But the rules are radically different. Where democratic coalitions are governed by elections, votes of confidence, and constitutional procedures, authoritarian coalitions operate through coercion, patronage, purges, and the ever-present threat of a coup.

Three key works illuminate these dynamics from different angles. [Acemoglu, Egorov, and Sonin (2008)](../../papers/acemoglu-coalition-formation-non-democracies/) build a formal game-theoretic model of ruling coalition dynamics in dictatorships, explaining the logic of purges and power consolidation. [Svolik (2012)](../../papers/svolik-politics-authoritarian-rule/) focuses on the fundamental commitment problem at the heart of authoritarian power-sharing — how dictators and their allies manage a relationship where violence is the ultimate arbiter. [Luebbert (1991)](../../papers/luebbert-liberalism-fascism-social-democracy/) takes a historical-comparative approach, showing how specific **class coalitions** in interwar Europe determined whether countries ended up as democracies or dictatorships in the first place.

Together, these works show that authoritarianism is not the absence of coalition politics but a different form of it, operating through distinct mechanisms and incentives.

## The Logic of Purges

[Acemoglu et al.](../../papers/acemoglu-coalition-formation-non-democracies/) demonstrate a counter-intuitive result: in authoritarian ruling coalitions, the **most powerful members are the most likely to be purged**. This is the opposite of democratic coalition logic, where powerful partners are valued for the votes and resources they bring.

In a dictatorship, power within the coalition is a threat. A strong ally can help you govern — but can also overthrow you. The model predicts that leaders will systematically eliminate capable associates, preferring weaker partners who pose less risk. This explains recurring patterns across authoritarian regimes:

| Regime | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Stalin's USSR | Purges of powerful Old Bolsheviks in the 1930s |
| Saddam's Iraq | Repeated elimination of military and party leaders |
| Mao's China | Cultural Revolution targeting senior party officials |
| Post-revolution Iran | Elimination of secular and leftist allies after 1979 |

The equilibrium is a ruling coalition of members too weak to challenge the leader individually — stable until an external shock disrupts the balance.

## The Commitment Problem and Institutional Solutions

[Svolik](../../papers/svolik-politics-authoritarian-rule/) identifies the deeper structural reason why authoritarian coalitions are so unstable: **no independent authority can enforce agreements among the key actors**. In democracies, courts and constitutions constrain leaders. In dictatorships, power-sharing agreements hold only as long as the balance of threat makes them self-enforcing.

This creates a persistent commitment problem. The dictator has incentives to grab more power at the coalition's expense, and the coalition's only deterrent is threatening a coup. Svolik distinguishes two equilibria that emerge from this dynamic:

| Type | Internal Dynamics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Contested dictatorship** | Ongoing bargaining; coup threat is credible | Many military regimes; early years of new dictatorships |
| **Established dictatorship** | Dictator has consolidated power; allies cannot credibly threaten a coup | Stalin after the purges; Mao after the Cultural Revolution |

The transition from contested to established dictatorship maps directly onto the purge logic described by Acemoglu et al. — the dictator systematically eliminates powerful coalition members until the remaining members are too weak to mount a challenge.

A key empirical finding is that **authoritarian institutions help stabilize ruling coalitions**. Regime parties, legislatures, and controlled elections are not democratic window-dressing — they serve as commitment devices that allow coalition members to monitor the dictator and reduce the need for costly rebellions. Dictatorships with legislatures have significantly longer-lived ruling coalitions than those without.

## Class Coalitions and Regime Outcomes

[Luebbert's](../../papers/luebbert-liberalism-fascism-social-democracy/) framework explains how different configurations of class alliances produced different regime types across interwar Europe. The key actors are the **urban middle class**, the **working class**, and the **peasantry**. The coalitions they formed (or failed to form) determined the political trajectory of entire nations:

| Coalition Configuration | Outcome | Countries |
|---|---|---|
| Unified middle class co-opts workers | Liberal democracy | Britain, France, Switzerland |
| Workers ally with peasantry | Social democracy | Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Czechoslovakia |
| Middle class and peasantry unite against workers | Fascism | Germany, Italy, Spain |

Luebbert's insight is that the **peasantry was the swing class**. Where rural voters went determined whether a country became democratic or authoritarian. This connects coalition theory directly to regime-level political outcomes.

## Connecting the Three Perspectives

These three works address different questions — Luebbert asks how authoritarian coalitions *come to power*, Acemoglu et al. ask *who gets purged* once they do, and Svolik asks how the ruling coalition *manages its ongoing relationship* — but they share a core insight: **coalition structure determines political outcomes**, whether those outcomes are policy positions, cabinet portfolios, or the survival of democracy itself.

The inter-class dynamics that Luebbert describes (which social groups align to seize power) set the stage. Once a regime takes control, the commitment problem that Svolik identifies kicks in, and the purge logic that Acemoglu et al. model determines how the ruling coalition evolves from a contested to an established dictatorship.
