---
title: Coalition Change
description: How coalitions of power shift, realign, and transform over time — from party realignments to power transitions between states.
---

Coalitions are not static. They form, persist for a time, and then *change* — sometimes gradually, sometimes in sudden, dramatic ruptures. This section covers the research on how and why coalitions of power shift over time, at scales ranging from domestic party systems to the global balance of power.

The common thread across these theories is that coalition change is driven by **structural shifts** — in economic power, demographic composition, or the distribution of threats — that make the existing coalition arrangement unstable. The specific mechanisms differ (electoral realignment, burden-sharing disputes, power transitions between great powers), but the underlying logic is consistent: when the conditions that supported a coalition change, the coalition must adapt or dissolve.

## Party Realignment: Punctuated Change in Democracies

[V.O. Key (1955)](../../papers/key-critical-elections/) introduced the concept of **critical elections** — elections that mark sharp, durable shifts in which social groups align with which party. Rather than evolving gradually, party coalitions appear to change through **punctuated equilibria**: long periods of stability interrupted by sudden reorganization.

The classic American realignments illustrate the pattern:

| Period | Trigger | Coalition Shift |
|---|---|---|
| 1860 | Civil War | South becomes Democratic; North becomes Republican |
| 1896 | Industrialization | Urban industry consolidates behind Republicans |
| 1932 | Great Depression | FDR's New Deal coalition (labor, urban ethnic groups, African Americans, the South) |
| 1960s–80s | Civil rights, cultural change | Southern whites shift Republican; educated suburbanites drift Democratic |

Key's framework suggests that coalition change in democracies is not random but follows a pattern: existing coalitions manage normal political conflict until a **crisis** exposes their inability to address a major issue. The crisis triggers realignment, and a new coalition structure stabilizes for a generation.

Walter Dean Burnham extended this into a cyclical theory (~30-36 year realignment cycles), though David Mayhew later argued the evidence for strict periodicity is weaker than claimed. The debate continues: is the current reshuffling of American party coalitions along education and urban/rural lines a "critical realignment" or something more gradual?

[Przeworski and Sprague (1986)](../../papers/przeworski-sprague-paper-stones/) identified a structural mechanism driving gradual coalition change within parties: the **electoral dilemma** of social democracy. Because the working class alone is rarely large enough to win elections, social democratic parties must broaden their coalition to include middle-class voters — but doing so dilutes their class identity and risks losing their base. This ongoing tension produces a slow, structural erosion of class-based coalitions, complementing Key's model of sudden realignment with a theory of **incremental coalition drift**.

Coalition change also occurs *within* parties. [Leiserson (1968)](../../papers/leiserson-factions-coalitions-japan/) showed that Japan's Liberal Democratic Party — which governed almost continuously from 1955 to 2009 — operated as a coalition system in miniature, with internal factions forming minimum winning coalitions to control the party presidency and bargaining over cabinet portfolios exactly as parties do in multi-party parliaments. Factional realignment within a dominant party can be as consequential as inter-party realignment.

## Burden-Sharing Disputes: Friction Within Alliances

[Olson and Zeckhauser (1966)](../../papers/olson-zeckhauser-economic-theory-alliances/) identified a structural source of coalition change in international alliances: the **free-rider problem**. In any alliance providing a collective good (like security), smaller members under-contribute while the largest member bears a disproportionate burden. This creates persistent internal tension — the "exploitation of the great by the small."

Over time, burden-sharing disputes can drive coalition change through several mechanisms:

| Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|
| Largest member demands reforms | US pressure on NATO allies to meet 2% GDP defense spending target |
| Smallest members resist increased contributions | European reluctance to raise defense spending through the 2000s–2010s |
| External shock temporarily resolves the dispute | Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine dramatically increased European defense spending |
| Largest member threatens withdrawal or restructuring | Periodic US threats to reduce NATO commitment |

The free-rider dynamic means that even stable, successful alliances face continuous internal pressure for change. Coalition evolution is not just about external threats but about the **internal distribution of costs and benefits**.

## Power Transitions: Systemic Realignment

[Organski's power transition theory (1958)](../../papers/organski-power-transition-theory/) operates at the largest scale: shifts in the global hierarchy of power. When a rising, dissatisfied great power approaches parity with the dominant state, the entire international coalition structure is at risk of transformation. The dominant power leads a coalition of satisfied states; the challenger may build a counter-coalition of dissatisfied ones.

The theory predicts that the moment of maximum danger — and maximum coalition instability — is the **transition point** where the challenger's power crosses that of the dominant state:

| Phase | Coalition Structure | Stability |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-transition** | Dominant power leads coalition of satisfied states | Stable |
| **Approaching parity** | Challenger builds counter-coalition; swing states hedge | Unstable |
| **Post-transition** | New dominant power restructures the international order | Re-stabilizing |

Historical examples include Germany's rise before both World Wars, the Cold War US-Soviet rivalry, and the current US-China dynamic. Each involved (or involves) a fundamental reshuffling of which states align with whom.

## Gradual Institutional Change

Not all coalition change is sudden. [Mahoney and Thelen (2010)](../../papers/mahoney-thelen-gradual-institutional-change/) identified four mechanisms by which coalitions reshape institutions **incrementally**, without a dramatic rupture: **displacement** (old rules replaced by new ones), **layering** (new rules added alongside old ones), **drift** (existing rules eroded as the environment changes), and **conversion** (existing rules reinterpreted for new purposes). Each mode reflects a different balance of power between coalitions defending the status quo and challengers seeking change. The framework shows that coalitions don't always need to win outright to reshape the political landscape — weaker coalitions can achieve gradual transformation through creative workarounds.

## Common Patterns

Across these very different domains, coalition change shares several features:

1. **Change is often non-linear.** Key's critical elections, Organski's power transitions, and Olson's burden-sharing crises all describe systems that appear stable until they suddenly aren't — though Mahoney and Thelen show that much consequential change happens gradually beneath the surface.
2. **Structural conditions set the stage; events provide the trigger.** Demographic shifts, economic growth, and capability changes create the *preconditions* for coalition realignment; specific crises or elections are the *catalysts*.
3. **New coalitions can be hard to predict in advance.** Which groups will align after a realignment depends on contingent factors (leadership decisions, the specific nature of the crisis) that structural theories alone cannot fully determine.
