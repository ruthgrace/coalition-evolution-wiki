---
title: Coalition Formation
description: Why and how coalitions come into existence — from parliamentary governments to international alliances to class-based political movements.
---

The question of *why particular coalitions form* is the oldest and most central puzzle in coalition theory. Given a set of actors with different resources, preferences, and positions, which subsets will join together? The research spans formal game theory, comparative politics, international relations, and historical sociology — and has produced a rich set of competing (and sometimes complementary) answers.

The theories surveyed here can be roughly organized by what they think drives coalition formation: **size** (Riker), **ideology** (De Swaan), **institutional structure** (Laver & Shepsle), **external threat** (Walt), or **class interest** (Luebbert).

## Size-Based Theories: Minimum Winning Coalitions

[Riker's *Theory of Political Coalitions* (1962)](../../papers/riker-theory-of-political-coalitions/) launched the formal study of coalition formation with the **size principle**: rational actors form coalitions that are just large enough to win, and no larger. Surplus members dilute the payoff. This prediction follows directly from modeling politics as an n-person zero-sum game.

The minimum winning coalition hypothesis set the agenda for the field, even though its empirical track record is mixed — oversized coalitions are common in practice. Subsequent theories can be understood as attempts to explain *why* coalitions deviate from minimum winning size.

## Policy-Based Theories: Ideology and Distance

[De Swaan (1973)](../../papers/de-swaan-policy-distance-theory/) shifted the focus from size to **policy distance**. Actors don't just want to win; they want the coalition's policy output to be as close as possible to their own ideal point. His **closed minimal range theory** predicts coalitions of ideologically adjacent parties spanning the smallest possible range on a policy scale.

| Theory | What Drives Formation | Key Prediction |
|---|---|---|
| **Riker (1962)** | Maximizing payoff share | Minimum winning coalition |
| **De Swaan (1973)** | Minimizing policy distance | Closed minimal range coalition |
| **Axelrod (1970)** | Minimizing ideological conflict | Minimal connected winning coalition |

De Swaan's approach achieved strong empirical results in Western European parliamentary systems, particularly during periods of political stability. It also laid the groundwork for the spatial models that now dominate formal coalition theory.

## Institutional Theories: Portfolios and Equilibria

[Laver and Shepsle (1996)](../../papers/laver-shepsle-making-breaking-governments/) built the most ambitious formal model, treating coalition formation as a problem of **portfolio allocation**. Their central premise: the minister who controls a policy domain *is* the policy in that domain. A coalition forms when a specific assignment of ministerial portfolios to parties constitutes an equilibrium — no parliamentary majority prefers an alternative allocation.

This approach integrates formation and termination into a single framework. Coalitions form at equilibrium points and collapse when shifts in party positions or seat distributions make a new equilibrium viable. It also places institutions (which party gets to propose, what the rules for no-confidence votes are) at the center of the analysis.

## Threat-Based Theories: International Alliances

[Walt's *Origins of Alliances* (1987)](../../papers/walt-origins-of-alliances/) explains coalition formation among states through **balance of threat theory**. States don't simply balance against the most powerful actor — they balance against the most *threatening* one, considering aggregate power, geographic proximity, offensive capabilities, and perceived intentions.

This resolved a key puzzle: during the Cold War, the US was more powerful than the USSR, yet more states allied with Washington. Balance of threat theory explains why: the Soviet Union was perceived as more threatening due to its geography, military posture, and ideology.

## Class-Based Theories: Historical Coalitions

[Moore (1966)](../../papers/moore-social-origins/) established the foundational argument that **class coalitions determine regime outcomes**. His comparative analysis of eight countries identified three "routes to the modern world": where a strong bourgeoisie challenged the landed aristocracy, democracy emerged; where a weak bourgeoisie allied with traditional elites, fascism resulted; where the peasantry was the revolutionary force, communism followed. His famous dictum — "no bourgeois, no democracy" — placed coalition formation at the center of the most consequential political outcomes.

[Luebbert (1991)](../../papers/luebbert-liberalism-fascism-social-democracy/) refined and extended Moore's framework for interwar Europe, showing how specific class alliances between the middle class, working class, and peasantry determined whether countries became liberal democracies, social democracies, or fascist dictatorships. [Przeworski and Sprague (1986)](../../papers/przeworski-sprague-paper-stones/) added a dynamic dimension: social democratic parties face a structural **electoral dilemma** because the working class alone is never large enough to win elections, forcing them to broaden their coalition to the middle class at the risk of alienating their base.

Together, these works show that coalition formation is not just about dividing office or policy spoils. At critical historical junctures, the coalitions that form (or fail to form) can determine the fate of entire political systems.

## Power and Governance: The Stationary Bandit

[Olson (1993)](../../papers/olson-stationary-bandit/) provided an elegant framework for understanding how governing coalitions form in the first place. His **stationary bandit** metaphor shows that a power-holder who monopolizes control over a territory develops an "encompassing interest" in its prosperity — leading even autocrats to provide public goods and restrain extraction. The framework implies that coalition formation is driven not just by ideology or threat but by the **structural incentives of governance**: the larger the share of society encompassed by the ruling coalition, the more public goods it provides. This logic connects the formation of ruling coalitions directly to governance quality.

## Beyond Formal Politics: Social Movement and Ethnic Coalitions

Coalition formation is not limited to parliaments, alliances, and class politics. [Arriola (2013)](../../papers/arriola-multi-ethnic-coalitions-africa/) showed that in Africa's ethnically divided, patronage-based systems, opposition leaders build **cross-ethnic coalitions** using private capital — financial liberalization creates independent capital holders who can fund opposition campaigns, enabling coalition-building outside the state's patronage network. The finding highlights that the structural conditions for coalition formation (access to resources) can matter as much as strategic calculations.

[Levi and Murphy (2006)](../../papers/levi-murphy-coalitions-contention-seattle/) analyzed the broad coalition behind the 1999 WTO protests in Seattle — the "teamsters and turtles" alliance of labor unions, environmentalists, and activist groups. They found that shared interests and compatible framing are **necessary but not sufficient** for coalition formation; partners must also be able to credibly commit to delivering resources and managing internal tensions. The fragility of the Seattle coalition (which largely dispersed after the event) illustrates why social movement coalitions are typically more episodic than their parliamentary or alliance counterparts.

## Cross-Cutting Themes

Despite their different domains and methods, these theories share several insights:

1. **Preferences matter as much as resources.** Pure size-based theories underperform those that incorporate ideology, policy, or threat perception.
2. **Formation is constrained by structure.** Institutional rules (Laver & Shepsle), system polarity (Walt), class organization (Moore, Luebbert), access to capital (Arriola), and governance incentives (Olson) all shape which coalitions are possible.
3. **Formation and maintenance are linked.** The same forces that bring a coalition together contain the seeds of its eventual dissolution — a theme explored further in the [coalition duration](../../sections/coalition-duration/) section.
